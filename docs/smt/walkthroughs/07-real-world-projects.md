## 1. The starting point (catalogue MIP, 1 month, maths teachers)
1. Decide the concepts to cover
2. Create a few real-life problem projects for those concepts
3. Introduce the project and timelines to students
4. Practise the concepts in class
5. Support students on the project once a week
6. Organise a student project showcase

## 2. What it already does well
Real projects tied to concepts, and a showcase.
**The gaps:** the **teacher** invents the "real-life" problems, so students are recipients · no structured teamwork · the audience is the school only · no link to adults who actually use this maths.

## 3. What was applied, and why

| Method | In plain words | Why here |
|---|---|---|
| **Interest** | The teacher sets 3–4 frames ("measure and compare", "track over time", "cost and budget"); each team proposes a real problem from home or the neighbourhood that fits one (cricket scores, shop prices, rainfall and crops, bus times) | "Real world" means more when it's *the students'* world |
| **Peer roles** | Teams of 4–5 with rotating roles (measurer, recorder, checker, presenter); a 5-minute peer feedback round each week | Everyone works on the maths, not just the strongest student |
| **Connections across settings** | Each team asks one adult who uses this maths at work (shopkeeper, farmer, tailor, conductor, a parent) one or two questions | Shows the maths is used outside school, by people students know |
| **Audience** | Parents or a younger class invited to the showcase; teams explain the maths, not just the result | A real audience, and explaining deepens understanding |

## 4. What was deliberately not done, and why
- **Concept practice (task 4) isn't reduced.** The project adds to teaching; it doesn't replace it.
- **No collection of sensitive household data.** Problems may use prices or scores, never family income.
- **No budget vote.** Students choose a problem, but nothing is scarce enough to need a budget ballot.

## 5. Before → after

| # | Before | After | Change |
|---|---|---|---|
| 1 | Decide concepts | Same | — |
| 2 | Teacher creates projects | **Teacher sets 3–4 frames; teams propose their own problem; teacher approves** | changed |
| 3 | Introduce project | Same, **with teams of 4–5 and rotating roles** | changed |
| 4 | Practise concepts | Same | — |
| 5 | — | **Each team asks one adult who uses this maths at work** | added |
| 6 | Weekly support | Same, **plus a 5-minute peer feedback round** | changed |
| 7 | Showcase | **Parents or a younger class invited; teams explain the maths** | changed |

7 tasks · 1 added · 1 month.

## 6. Safety and fairness rules
Mixed-ability, mixed-gender teams · roles rotate weekly (so nobody is always "the decorator") · adult interviews done with a family member or on a teacher-accompanied walk · no photos of children online without written parental consent.

## 7. Teacher card

> # Real World Projects: students pick the problem
> **Who runs it:** maths teacher · **Time:** 1 month · **Cost:** nil
> **Goal:** Students apply maths to practical problems. *Added:* each team works on a problem it chose and explains it to a family audience.
>
> ## Steps
> 1. Decide the concepts.
> 2. Set 3–4 project frames; each team proposes a real problem that fits; approve it. *Remember:* no family income or other sensitive data.
> 3. Introduce the projects; teams of 4–5 with rotating roles.
> 4. Practise the concepts in class as usual.
> 5. Each team asks one adult who uses this maths at work one or two questions.
> 6. Weekly support, plus a 5-minute "one thing that works, one question" round between teams.
> 7. Showcase with parents or a younger class; each team explains the maths.
>
> ## How you'll know it worked
> - Every team works on a problem it proposed
> - At least 80% of students can explain the concept in their project
> - At least one family member per 3 students attends (or a younger class does)
>
> ## What changed from the original (and why)
> | Step | Change | Why |
> |---|---|---|
> | 2 | Students propose the problem | It's their real world, not the teacher's guess at it |
> | 3, 6 | Rotating roles and peer feedback | Everyone does the maths |
> | 5, 7 | An adult who uses this maths; a family audience | Learning connects beyond the classroom |
>

## 8. What to watch for
Whether student-proposed problems still hit the chosen concepts (the approval step guards this) · whether concept mastery holds (the indicator measures it).
