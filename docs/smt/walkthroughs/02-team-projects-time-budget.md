# Walkthrough 2 — Teaching project teams to finish on time

> A **reference answer** written by hand, not tool output. The starting plan is a **seed** authored for this example. Technical version: [evals/golden/time-knapsack.md](../../../evals/golden/time-knapsack.md).

| | |
|---|---|
| **Runs it** | Subject teacher (e.g. Class 10 science) |
| **Time** | One project cycle, 3–4 weeks; the routine itself is 2 periods + 5-minute weekly check-ins |
| **Cost** | Nil |
| **Methods applied** | Knapsack-style **reasoning** about a time budget (borrowed, not labelled PB). Connected Learning: interest (moonshot ideas), peer roles, production. |
| **Ruled out** | **Calling it participatory budgeting**: a team dividing its own time isn't a community allocating a shared resource |

## 1. The starting point
Teams of 5 get a project, list what they plan to build, work on it, and submit. Each team has about **20 hours** in total before the deadline, and teams routinely plan more than they can finish, so they end up rushing or missing the deadline.

## 2. What it already does well
Real team projects with a product, a rubric and a presentation.
**The gap:** no step makes teams match their plans to the time they have.

## 3. What was applied, and why

| Method | In plain words | Why here |
|---|---|---|
| **Time as a budget** (reasoning adapted from knapsack voting) | Each feature gets an hours estimate; the team picks features that fit the hours | There's a hard cap (20 hours) and more ideas than fit; it's exactly the trade-off knapsack voting makes visible |
| **Lock the must-haves first** | Graded rubric items are included before any vote | So a team can't vote away what it's assessed on |
| **Plan to 80%** | Plan 16 of 20 hours; the rest is buffer | Estimates are always uncertain |
| **Justify every cut** | "We chose ___ over ___ because ___" | The learning is in weighing trade-offs, not just in cutting |
| **Interest and peer roles** (Connected Learning) | Every student adds an idea, including one "moonshot"; owners are tied to tasks | Everyone gets a stake, and nobody becomes the default worker |

## 4. What was deliberately not done, and why
- **Not called participatory budgeting.** That needs a group allocating a *shared public* resource with a public outcome. Here five students divide their own time, so it's prioritisation. The plan says "adapted from knapsack voting" instead.
- **Hours aren't treated like money.** Not everyone can do every task, so estimates are tied to owners and each student's load is capped.

## 5. Before → after

| Before | After |
|---|---|
| Announce topic, rubric, deadline | Same, **plus the teacher sets the 20-hour budget and lists the must-have rubric items** |
| Form teams | Same |
| Teams list what they plan to build | **List every feature** (each student adds one; include moonshots) → **estimate hours and owner** for each |
| Work on the project | **Prioritise within 16 hours:** must-haves first, then each member ticks optional features within the remaining hours; fund the most-ticked; write one sentence per cut; teacher approves the scope |
| Weekly check | **Hours tracker** plus a **mid-point re-scope**: at 50% of the hours, drop the lowest-ranked optional feature if behind |
| Submit and present | Same, plus a 10-minute class reflection on estimated vs. actual hours |

## 6. Fairness rules
No student over 1.25× an equal share of hours · roles not split by gender stereotype (e.g. girls always on decoration) · the scope commitment signed by all members.

## 7. Teacher card

> # Finishing on time: scoping within a time budget
> **Who runs it:** subject teacher · **Time:** one project cycle (2 periods + weekly 5-minute check-ins) · **Cost:** nil
> **Goal:** Teams plan within a fixed time budget, finish on time, and can explain what they chose not to build.
>
> ## Steps
> 1. Set the team time budget and list the must-have rubric items.
> 2. *Period 1:* Teams list all features and estimate hours and an owner for each. *Students:* everyone adds one idea.
> 3. *Period 2:* Must-haves first, then each member picks optional features within the hours left; write one sentence per cut; you approve. *Remember:* plan to 80% of the hours.
> 4. Weekly 5-minute check of the hours tracker; at the half-way point, drop the lowest-ranked optional feature if behind.
> 5. After submission: a 10-minute reflection on estimates vs. actual.
>
> ## How you'll know it worked
> - At least 80% of teams submit on time
> - Every team has a signed scope with justified cuts
> - No student logs more than 1.25× an equal share of hours
>
> ## What changed from the original (and why)
> | Step | Change | Why |
> |---|---|---|
> | 2–3 | Teams cost features in hours and choose within the budget | So plans match the time available |
> | 3 | Must-haves locked first; every cut justified | So graded work isn't dropped, and students practise trade-offs |
> | 4 | Mid-point re-scope | Estimates slip; cut early rather than rush |
>

## 8. What to watch for
Whether teams estimate realistically the first time (the reflection helps the next cycle) · whether the load check is actually done.
