# Seven transformers clear the evidence bar

Seven interventions clear a strict cutoff. Each is backed by causal evidence from classrooms that resemble Indian government schools, is likely to produce a gain worth having, is uncontroversial, and can be run on paper by a head teacher or teacher in 2–8 weeks. They form two tiers. **Tier 1 consists of four structural transformers: (1) level-based grouping in a protected daily slot, Teaching-at-the-Right-Level style; (2) in-school remedial tutoring of lagging children by local volunteers, or by trained older pupils where no volunteers are available; (3) a daily explicit akshara-sound and fluency block; and (4) a head-teacher fidelity loop around state-supplied structured-pedagogy materials.** Each has been tested in low-resource public-school systems with low baseline learning and large classes, in India, Kenya, Zambia and South Africa. **Tier 2 consists of three portable classroom routines: daily low-stakes retrieval quizzing, whole-class task-focused feedback via slates or exit tickets, and maths representations built from local objects.** Their evidence is mostly from the US and UK, but the mechanism is device-free and does not depend on small classes or extra staff. They sit at the margin, just above the cutoff. Expectations should be modest. Large preregistered trials average about **0.06 SD** ([Lortie-Forgues & Inglis 2019](https://eprints.whiterose.ac.uk/141754/)), and Kraft calls 0.05–0.19 SD a "medium" effect ([Kraft 2020](https://edworkingpapers.com/ai19-10)). So a realistic target is **about 0.05–0.15 SD on an independent test for Tier 1 and 0–0.10 SD for Tier 2**, not the 0.5–0.7 SD that toolkits and Hattie's tables imply. The project's existing frameworks score near the bottom as *learning* levers: Connected Learning and participatory budgeting have no causal learning-outcome evidence, and UCWhittle is an allocation algorithm. Participatory budgeting can still play a useful part, as a way to choose among shortlisted transformers. The finding that matters most for the catalogue is this: the most common head-teacher MIP pattern, "assess the school, write a plan, report", produced **zero learning effect at scale** in a large Indian RCT ([Muralidharan & Singh, RISE WP 20/056](https://riseprogramme.org/sites/default/files/2020-11/RISE_WP-056_Muralidharan_Singh.pdf)).

## Large trials shrink effects to about 0.06 SD, so short projects should aim at 0.05–0.15

Every ranking below is anchored to the effect sizes that rigorous, scaled studies actually produce, not to the headline numbers in syntheses. Kraft built his benchmarks from causal studies with standardised achievement outcomes: **below 0.05 SD is small, 0.05–0.19 is medium, and 0.20 or more is large** ([Kraft 2020, EdWorkingPapers](https://edworkingpapers.com/ai19-10); [CGD summary](https://www.cgdev.org/node/3128013)). His 2023 follow-up reports that **36% of RCT effects on standardised achievement fall below 0.05 SD**, and he concludes that "education interventions often fail" ([Kraft blog](https://www.matthewakraft.com/the-effect-size-benchmark-that-matters-most-education-interventions-often-fail)). Kraft's dataset size (about 1,942 effects from 747 RCTs) is **[unverified]**. Lortie-Forgues and Inglis pooled 141 large EEF and NCEE trials covering **1,222,024 students** and found a **mean of about 0.06 SD**, with many trials too imprecise to be informative ([White Rose eprint](https://eprints.whiterose.ac.uk/141754/)). A UCL comment computes a weighted mean of **0.04** ([UCL](https://discovery.ucl.ac.uk/id/eprint/10101577/1/Informing%20better%20trial%20design%20-%20A%20Technical%20Comment%20on%20Lortie-Forgues%20and%20Inglis%202019.pdf)). The gap probably reflects weighted versus unweighted averaging, but this is unresolved.

The gap between those averages and the popular syntheses is systematic. Cheung and Slavin's analysis of 645 studies found that **small-sample studies show roughly twice the effects of large ones**. Researcher-made tests, quasi-experiments and published studies each inflate effects independently ([bestevidence.org summary](https://bestevidence.org/2021/02/07/methods-methodological-features-and-effect-sizes/)). In Kraft's coaching meta-analysis, programmes with 50 or fewer teachers had about **3x the achievement effect** of programmes with 100 or more ([Learning Forward summary](https://learningforward.org/journal/august-2018-vol-39-no-4/the-impact-of-coaching-on-teacher-practice-and-student-achievement/)). The EEF Toolkit's months-to-SD conversion puts "+6 months" at **0.45–0.52 SD** ([EEF Toolkit Guide](https://d2tic4wvo1iusb.cloudfront.net/production/documents/toolkit/toolkit_guide_2025_v3.0.0.pdf)). That is about eight times the large-trial average.

The Indian evidence shows the same shrinkage within a single programme. TaRL produced **0.70 SD** in Uttar Pradesh, where Pratham volunteers ran 40-day in-school camps. It produced **0.15 SD** in Haryana, where government teachers taught a dedicated hour with mentoring. It produced **nothing** in Bihar and Uttarakhand, where teachers were trained but given no protected time ([NBER w22746](https://www.nber.org/papers/w22746)). Zambia's government-run national version gained **0.10 SD in literacy and 0.15 SD in numeracy over two years**. Targeted maths skills rose 0.40 SD while non-targeted skills stayed flat, and only about a third of pupils attended on a typical day ([J-PAL Zambia](https://www.povertyactionlab.org/evaluation/targeting-foundational-skills-improve-learning-scale-zambia)). This report's planning assumption is an inference from those figures. A school-run 4–8 week version of a Tier 1 transformer should be expected to deliver **0.05–0.15 SD overall on an independent measure**, with larger movement on the skills it targets. The notes put defensible planning gains for Tier 2 classroom routines at **0.1–0.3 SD on a pre-specified unit test**, and well under 0.10 SD on an independent test. Management projects should first be judged on intermediate outcomes: attendance, time-on-task and children's level movement. Measurement matters as much as the intervention. A teacher-made post-test roughly doubles the apparent gain, so every transformer should carry an ASER-style oral assessment at baseline and endline.

## Reputation predicts study quality, not truth, so it only breaks ties

The brief asked for researcher reputation as a third criterion. The evidence on reputation supports a narrow role for it, as a confidence modifier and tie-breaker, for four reasons. First, replication in education is almost absent. Only **0.13% of articles in the top 100 education journals were replications** (221 of 164,589) ([AERA, Makel & Plucker 2014](https://www.aera.net/Newsroom/Recent-AERA-Research/Facts-Are-More-Important-Than-Novelty-Replication-in-the-Education-Sciences)). Citations therefore record attention, not confirmed validity. Second, every accepted evidence hierarchy grades the study and the body of evidence, never the author: EEF padlocks, WWC standards, GRADE and 3ie/GEEAP ([EEF Toolkit Guide](https://d2tic4wvo1iusb.cloudfront.net/production/documents/toolkit/toolkit_guide_2025_v3.0.0.pdf); [3ie SR7](https://3ieimpact.org/SRS7)). Third, several of the most prominent names here earned their standing in fields that produce no K-12 learning-outcome trials. Ito is a cultural anthropologist whose Digital Youth Project was ethnographic ([GIA](https://www.giarts.org/node/1267)). Goel and Tambe are computer scientists who work on voting mechanisms and AI allocation ([Stanford profile, background link not opened](https://profiles.stanford.edu/ashish-goel); [Harvard Teamcore, background link not opened](https://teamcore.seas.harvard.edu/people/milind-tambe)). Scoring reputation additively would lift their frameworks on credentials that say nothing about classroom gains.

Fourth, high reputation coexists with conflicts of interest and with flagship results that later had to be corrected. Hattie's Visible Learning has a consultancy arm ([Education Week](https://www.edweek.org/leadership/opinion-john-hattie-isnt-wrong-you-are-misusing-his-research/2018/06)). His top-ranked "collective teacher efficacy" figure, **d = 1.57, comes from one dissertation meta-analysing 26 correlational studies**, and has since been revised to 1.49 and then 1.39 ([Corwin FAQ](https://corwinvisiblelearningsolutions.sagepub.com/support/solutions/articles/7000090507-what-is-the-evidence-for-the-1-57-effect-size-for-collective-teacher-efficacy-)). Slavin evaluated a programme he co-founded. Piper led and evaluated Tusome. Pratham co-authors TaRL papers ([NBER w22931](https://www.nber.org/papers/w22931)). Several GEEAP panellists authored the RCTs the panel rates ([GEEAP 2023](https://documents1.worldbank.org/curated/en/099008110232520373/pdf/IDU-9401bfdd-f4ff-486b-84b4-570be4f65543.pdf)). A Nobel-winning lab's flagship deworming result was partly corrected on reanalysis, and the shared data that made the correction possible counts in the lab's favour ([GiveWell](https://blog.givewell.org/2015/07/24/new-deworming-reanalyses-and-cochrane-review/)). A search snippet reported a 2026 University of Melbourne investigation of Hattie. It is **[unverified]**, its scope is unknown, and it played no part in any score.

Reputation does carry one legitimate signal: *expected* study quality. So the scheme scores credibility on three things: (a) a record of preregistered, open-data trials; (b) independence from the developer; and (c) whether the researcher's earlier flagship results replicated. Citation counts and h-indices were **not retrieved**, because Google Scholar blocks automated access. They are not scored. They should be filled in by hand, with the date checked, and used only to confirm that a researcher is established.

### Context relevance is judged by similarity to Indian public schools, not by country of origin

Where a study was done neither qualifies nor disqualifies it. **Evidence from outside India counts fully when its context resembles an Indian government school, and Indian origin earns no bonus.** The context score (C) is judged against eight features, and the study's own features are cited for each score:

1. public or government schools
2. low-resource classrooms
3. large classes
4. multilingual pupils, or instruction not in the home language
5. low baseline learning levels
6. limited devices
7. constrained teacher capacity
8. a low- or middle-income setting

Evidence from high-income countries (EEF, WWC) still counts. It scores high or medium when the mechanism is portable and the conditions match, as with device-free classroom routines that work in large classes. It scores low when it depends on high-income infrastructure such as tutoring budgets, devices, small classes, specialist staff or heavy professional development.

### The scoring formula, with weights you can change

The credibility notes propose weights that sum to 95%, so the composite divides by 0.95 to stay on a 1–4 scale:

**Composite = (0.35·E + 0.25·S + 0.20·C + 0.15·F) / 0.95 − D + 0.10·R**

| Symbol | Dimension | Scale | Weight |
|---|---|---|---|
| E | Evidence quality, GRADE-style: design, independence, replication, sample | 4 High / 3 Moderate / 2 Low / 1 Very low | 35% |
| S | Best-quality *verified* estimate on learning (not the largest), in Kraft bands | 1 = <0.05 or none; 2–3 = 0.05–0.19; 4 = ≥0.20 | 25% |
| C | Context similarity, judged against the eight features above | 4 = most features match and the mechanism is portable; 3 = several match, or a high-income device-free routine whose conditions match; 2 = partial match, or the mechanism needs skills or materials that are scarce here; 1 = depends on high-income infrastructure (tutoring budgets, devices, small classes), or lab only | 20% |
| F | Cost and MIP feasibility: offline, paper-first, head teacher or teacher, 2–8 weeks | 4 very easy … 1 needs devices, posts or state money | 15% |
| D | Controversy deduction | A = 0; B (direction holds, size contested) = 0.5; C (core claim unsupported) = 1.5 | deduction |
| R | Researcher or organisation credibility | −1 / 0 / +1 | modifier |

The 0.10 multiplier on R is deliberate. The smallest one-step change on any primary dimension moves the composite by at least 0.158 (one step on F is 0.15/0.95). Reputation therefore **can never overturn a real difference in evidence, effect, context or cost**. It can only separate items that are otherwise tied. The notes' guard-rail also applies: reputation cannot lift Very-low evidence above Low.

The composite has one flaw on its own: it **rewards certainty whatever the direction of the effect**. A large, well-run null trial earns high E and high C, and India's school self-assessment programme scores 3.31 despite zero effect. Four gates close that gap:

- **G1, significance:** S ≥ 2, meaning a verified, realistic gain of at least 0.05 SD.
- **G2, feasibility:** F ≥ 3.
- **G3, non-controversial:** D = 0.
- **G4, evidence:** E ≥ 3.

**The shortlist cutoff is a composite of at least 3.00 with all four gates passed.**

### Why targeted instruction wins on all three criteria

Level-based grouping is the only option at or near the top on all three of the brief's criteria. Its context score rests on matching features, not on Indian origin, because the evidence spans three countries' public systems. On evidence, there have been **six randomised evaluations in seven Indian states**, which J-PAL calls some of the largest effects rigorously measured in education ([J-PAL TaRL](https://www.povertyactionlab.org/scale-ups/teaching-right-level)). Kenya's tracking RCT found **+0.14 SD at 18 months and +0.16 SD a year after tracking ended**, with both high and low achievers gaining ([AER, Duflo, Dupas & Kremer 2011](https://econpapers.repec.org/RePEc:aea:aecrev:v:101:y:2011:i:5:p:1739-74)). Zambia's national government scale-up is covered above. On reputation, the team has a record of trials that replicated across sites. GEEAP rates targeted instruction a "Great Buy" ([GEEAP 2023](https://documents1.worldbank.org/curated/en/099008110232520373/pdf/IDU-9401bfdd-f4ff-486b-84b4-570be4f65543.pdf)). Angrist et al. estimate it yields **up to 3–4 learning-adjusted years of schooling per $100** in India ([Angrist et al. 2023](https://www.wwhge.org/wp-content/uploads/2024/05/BSG-WP-2023-057-Improve-Education-Outcomes-small.pdf)). The evidence is also specific about how it fails. **Training alone or materials alone did not work.** What worked was clear goals, use of data, mentoring and regular review ([J-PAL TaRL](https://www.povertyactionlab.org/scale-ups/teaching-right-level)). Those are head-teacher tasks.

Volunteer tutoring ranks second. Balsakhi tutors pulled lagging Grade 3–4 children out of class in municipal schools. School-level scores rose **0.14 SD in year 1 and 0.28 SD in year 2**, with **about 0.6 SD for tutored children**, at a corrected cost of **$0.67 per 0.1 SD** ([J-PAL](https://www.povertyactionlab.org/print/pdf/node/1415?lang=en)). One set of notes verified these figures against J-PAL and another lists them as unverified. Gains faded after the programme ended. Volunteer reading camps in Jaunpur also substantially improved reading for the children who attended ([NBER w14311](https://www.nber.org/system/files/working_papers/w14311/w14311.pdf)). The broader tutoring meta-analysis pools **0.29 SD across 89 RCTs**, finding in-school tutoring stronger than after-school and non-professional tutors weaker ([AERJ 2024](https://journals.sagepub.com/doi/10.3102/00028312231208687); [NBER w27476](https://www.nber.org/papers/w27476)). That pooled figure depends partly on paid-tutor budgets, a high-income condition. That is why T2's context score rests on Balsakhi's low-cost local tutors rather than on the meta-analysis. Structured peer tutoring (**+6 months, 4 padlocks** ([EEF](https://educationendowmentfoundation.org.uk/education-evidence/teaching-learning-toolkit/peer-tutoring))) scores C = 3: it turns large classes into a resource, but needs tutor training. It is folded into T2 as the fallback where no volunteers exist.

Foundational reading is third. WWC rates phonemic awareness and decoding **"Strong"** for K-3, and multisyllabic decoding and fluency-building **"Strong"** for Grades 4–9 ([WWC guide 21](https://ies.ed.gov/ncee/wwc/Docs/PracticeGuide/wwc_foundationalreading_070516.pdf); [WWC guide 29](https://ies.ed.gov/ncee/wwc/practiceguide/29)). The EEF rates phonics **+5 months on 4 padlocks** ([EEF phonics](https://educationendowmentfoundation.org.uk/education-evidence/teaching-learning-toolkit/phonics)). GEEAP's 2025 reading report reviewed about 120 LMIC studies, about 50 of them from Africa. **All eight high-performing large-scale programmes used explicit subskill instruction, including phonics-based decoding** ([World Bank, Oct 2025](https://www.worldbank.org/en/news/press-release/2025/10/30/new-report-offers-evidence-based-solutions-to-address-global-literacy-crisis-among-children)). That LMIC base matches the low-baseline, multilingual features, and it is why C = 4. The UK "synthetic phonics only" debate is real ([Durham, Torgerson et al. 2019](https://durham-repository.worktribe.com/output/1339781)). The item is still graded A, because the transformer is framed as explicit sound-symbol instruction inside a balanced programme, the position both sides accept. No meta-analysis of phonics in alphasyllabic Indian scripts was found.

Structured pedagogy itself is a Great Buy. It averages about **0.23 SD in language and 0.14 SD in maths**, and Tusome reached 0.63–0.76 SD in Kenyan public schools ([World Bank 2021](https://thedocs.worldbank.org/en/doc/7b72550a6aec51f4e6fbbd31199d7b3b-0200022021/original/SuccessfulTeachers-Oct-5.pdf)). In South Africa, on-site coaching gave **0.232 SD** in reading ([RISE WP-024](https://riseprogramme.org/sites/default/files/2020-11/RISE_WP-024_Cilliers_TeachingPractice.pdf)). Those contexts match closely. But head-teacher observation is a weaker copy of the trained coaches that were studied, so evidence quality is graded down to E = 3, and the item lands at 3.21. It applies only where NIPUN Bharat or state materials are already in classrooms. No Indian RCT of NIPUN materials had published results; one is registered in Uttar Pradesh ([AEA registry 17638](https://www.socialscienceregistry.org/trials/17638)).

### Portable classroom routines clear the bar, but only just

Retrieval practice, whole-class feedback and maths representations qualify because the context rule credits portable mechanisms. Dunlosky et al. rated only practice testing and distributed practice "high utility" ([APS](https://www.psychologicalscience.org/news/releases/which-study-strategies-make-the-grade.html)). WWC rates quizzing to re-expose key content **"Strong"** ([WWC guide 1](https://ies.ed.gov/ncee/wwc/practiceguide/1)). Of 50 experiments run in real classrooms, **57% showed medium or large benefits** ([x-mol record of Agarwal et al. 2021](https://www.x-mol.com/paper/1371679026699517952)). That work needs no devices, extra staff or small classes; a slate quiz works with 60 pupils. Its samples, though, were mostly secondary and undergraduate pupils without low baselines. No primary-school classroom RCT was verified ([TES](https://www.tes.com/magazine/teaching-learning/general/why-retrieval-practice-safe-bet-teachers)), so C is 3, not 4. The best quantitative re-estimate for formative assessment is **about 0.20 SD**, not the 0.4–0.7 often attributed to Black and Wiliam ([NCME erratum](https://ncme.org/wp-content/uploads/2025/10/Erratum_to_Kingston___Nash__Vol__34__No__2_.pdf)). It scores C = 3 only in its whole-class form, because individual written feedback does not scale to large classes. WWC rates all six practices for struggling elementary maths students "Strong", including the number line and concrete representations ([WWC](https://ies.ed.gov/ncee/wwc/Docs/PracticeGuide/WWC-PraxGuide-Elementary-Math-Summary-508c.pdf)). Those practices target strugglers, a close match for low baselines, and they work with sticks and stones.

These three items sit at 3.16–3.26, so one step down on any dimension drops them below the cutoff. That is the honest reason for putting them in a second tier. Reciprocal teaching shows how the context rule discriminates. Its standardised-test median is reportedly **0.32 SD** **[unverified]** ([Rosenshine & Meister 1994 DOI](https://doi.org/10.3102/00346543064004479)), but it assumes pupils can already decode. Many pupils in low-baseline classrooms cannot, so it scores C = 2 (2.95) and waits behind T3. Metacognition falls short for a similar reason: it depends on skilled teacher modelling, and the EEF itself says its +8 months is "difficult to realise in practice" ([EEF](https://educationendowmentfoundation.org.uk/education-evidence/teaching-learning-toolkit/metacognition-and-self-regulation)). Re-weighting toward evidence and effect (40/30/10/15) lifts reciprocal teaching to 3.05 and leaves metacognition at 2.89. Under that weighting, every shortlisted item stays above 3.0.

### The 60% head-teacher catalogue holds the strongest null evidence

Management, community and infrastructure projects make up most of the catalogue, and this is where the evidence is most sobering. Madhya Pradesh rolled out school assessment, an improvement plan and supervisor follow-up across **1,774 schools, with no effect on learning at 3–4 or 15–18 months**. A non-experimental evaluation of the national Shaala Siddhi version, covering more than 600,000 schools, also found no learning impact, and officials described the exercise as paperwork ([J-PAL](https://www.povertyactionlab.org/node/4841173); [VoxDev](https://voxdev.org/topic/education/improving-public-sector-management-scale)). In Jaunpur, informing communities about their committee roles and training them in a reading test had **no impact** ([NBER w14311](https://www.nber.org/system/files/working_papers/w14311/w14311.pdf)). GEEAP classes textbooks, buildings, grants and libraries as **"Bad Buys" when nothing else changes** ([GEEAP 2023](https://documents1.worldbank.org/curated/en/099008110232520373/pdf/IDU-9401bfdd-f4ff-486b-84b4-570be4f65543.pdf)). Angrist et al. find a zero median effect for community school-management training, inputs alone and general teacher training, and **null effects for more than half the interventions reviewed** ([Angrist et al.](https://www.wwhge.org/wp-content/uploads/2024/05/BSG-WP-2023-057-Improve-Education-Outcomes-small.pdf)). The Campbell review of school-based management finds gains mainly in middle-income settings, none in low-income countries, and smaller gains where parents have little education ([3ie](https://3ieimpact.org/node/142)). Bloom et al. show that management quality is *associated* with outcomes and that India ranks near the bottom ([NBER w20667](https://www.nber.org/papers/w20667)), but the MP trial failed to turn that association into a causal gain. The lesson is structural. **Every head-teacher planning task should produce a classroom-facing routine**, such as protecting the level-group hour, reviewing level movement weekly, or following up absent children. A plan with no routine attached is an excluded MIP.

## The project's existing frameworks rank as lenses, not learning levers

**Connected Learning** scores **1.32**. Ito's framework grew out of the MacArthur Digital Youth Project, an ethnographic study that interviewed more than 800 young people ([GIA](https://www.giarts.org/node/1267)). Its closest implementation, Pinkard's Digital Youth Network, served Chicago middle-schoolers through digital media and was studied with mixed methods, not a trial ([DYN](https://www.digitalyouthnetwork.org/about-us)). The notes find **no learning-outcome RCTs**, and the studied context depends on devices and out-of-school programmes, so C = 1. Ito's standing in anthropology cannot lift Very-low learning evidence. The framework stays defensible as a **design lens for engagement**, for example choosing texts or tutor pairings around children's interests. It should not be cited as the reason a MIP will raise scores. Its nearest causal cousin, structured collaborative learning, rates **+5 months on only 2 padlocks** ([EEF collaborative learning](https://educationendowmentfoundation.org.uk/education-evidence/teaching-learning-toolkit/collaborative-learning-approaches)).

**Participatory budgeting** also scores 1.32 when treated as an intervention. The search found **no RCT or quasi-experiment linking student councils, student voice or school participatory budgeting to learning**. It found only descriptive work and one non-randomised civic-outcomes evaluation ([CIRCLE WP70](https://circle.Tufts.Edu/sites/default/files/2019-12/WP70_FiveYearEvaluationofaComprehensiveHighSchoolCivicEngagementInitiative_2010.pdf); [EEA Grants](https://eeagrants.org/node/20075)). Goel's work concerns voting mechanisms ([pbstanford, background link not opened](https://pbstanford.org/)). PB's proper role is as a *procedure for choosing*, and a procedure is only as good as its ballot. An open ballot can fund library corners and smart-class kits, which are GEEAP Bad Buys. **A ballot restricted to the shortlisted transformers and their costed variants makes PB a legitimate way to choose among options that all have evidence behind them.** The nearest causal evidence on community voice points the same way: in Indonesia, linking the school committee to the village council beat training and grants ([GSDRC](https://gsdrc.org/document-library/improving-educational-quality-through-enhancing-community-participation-results-from-a-randomised-field-experiment-in-indonesia/)). That linkage arm's 0.17–0.22 SD figure is **[unverified]**.

**UCWhittle** scores **1.00**, which confirms the decision to set it aside. It is a resource-allocation algorithm from the AI-for-social-good tradition. The notes record Tambe's maternal-health work with ARMMAN but **no education-outcome evidence** ([Harvard Teamcore, background link not opened](https://teamcore.seas.harvard.edu/people/milind-tambe)). It decides *who* gets a scarce intervention. The ranking shows that the bigger decision is *which* intervention. The shortlist's own targeting rule, "the children below level", comes from a two-minute oral test.

## Figures to verify before publication

The Balsakhi figures (0.14/0.28/0.6 SD) are verified in one set of notes and unverified in another. The Mindspark sample (about 619 students, 4.5 months) is **[unverified]**. The Rajasthan evidence conflicts. One note cites an in-school follow-up with a **null maths effect at 9 months** ([AEA Registry 2459](https://www.socialscienceregistry.org/trials/2459)). Another cites a scaled study finding **+0.22 in maths and +0.20 in Hindi** ([NBER w34205](https://www.nber.org/papers/w34205)). These may be different studies. Also **[unverified]**:

- the reciprocal-teaching medians (0.32/0.88)
- peer tutoring's 0.40 (Cohen et al.)
- the phonics estimates: d = 0.41 (Ehri) and 0.27 (Torgerson 2006)
- Stockard's Direct Instruction pooled d
- Cipriano's SEL effect, g ≈ 0.22
- Sisk's mindset effect, d ≈ 0.08
- the Indonesia linkage effect
- the Madagascar information effect, about 0.2 SD; verifying it would move returns information onto the shortlist
- the five-year performance-pay effects
- Kraft's dataset size

The EEF figures were read from archived snapshots of the live Toolkit (August–October 2026), and other sources quote different versions (for example, +6 versus +8 months for feedback). GEEAP's 2023 tiers may have a newer edition ([GEEAP page](https://www.worldbank.org/en/brief/2025/10/30/global-education-evidence-advisory-panel)). Citation counts and h-indices remain blank. No systematic Retraction Watch search was run. Across all the notes, RCT evidence for Grades 9–12 in contexts like these is thin, so the shortlist is strongest for Grades 1–8.

## Conclusion

The ranking reverses an intuition the catalogue invites. The head teacher's lever is not planning. It is control of **time, grouping and data**: protecting an hour, regrouping children by what they can do, and checking weekly whether they moved. That bundle separated TaRL's successes from its failures in public systems in India and Zambia. So the most valuable revision to the 60% of MIPs owned by head teachers is to make them owners of T1, T2 and T4, not authors of plans. Teacher MIPs then supply the *content* of that time through T3 and the portable routines T5–T7. With that, the two halves of the catalogue work as one design. Judging relevance by context similarity rather than geography is what lets well-tested, device-free classroom routines from England and the US in. It also keeps out evidence that relies on tutoring budgets, devices or small classes.

The second implication concerns honesty about size. Promising 0.05–0.15 SD sounds modest. Measured against Kraft's benchmarks and a large-trial average of 0.06, it is a solidly medium effect that most programmes never reach. Holding MIPs to that bar, on an independent oral test, makes the catalogue's own results interpretable. A participatory-budgeting ballot limited to the shortlist then gives communities a real choice that cannot land on a Bad Buy. Each school-run cycle also adds evidence that can lift the marginal Tier 2 routines, and the waiting list, into firm standing.
