# School-level, teacher-development, leadership, family-engagement and attendance/dropout interventions: evidence inventory

Scope: primary, middle and high school; ranking for Indian government-school micro-improvement projects (MIPs: 2–8 weeks, low cost, mostly led by the head teacher or SMC).
Verification key: **[V]** = figure checked this session against an abstract, a J-PAL/3ie/IES summary or the paper. **[UNVERIFIED]** = from prior knowledge, not re-checked this session. The report writer should confirm these before quoting them or drop them.
"MIP-feasible?" = whether a head teacher or SMC can run it in 2–8 weeks at low cost (HT/SMC), or whether it needs system policy or money (SYSTEM).

---

## 1. Teacher coaching, lesson study, PLCs, collective teacher efficacy

### Takeaway
Coaching has the strongest causal base of the teacher-development options: 0.18 SD on achievement in a meta-analysis of 60 causal studies. Effects fall sharply at scale, and almost all of the evidence comes from the US. Lesson study and structured peer observation came out null in large EEF RCTs. The "collective teacher efficacy = 1.57" claim is correlational and inflated, and should not be used to rank anything.

### Cited Findings
- **Coaching meta-analysis.** Kraft, Blazar & Hogan (2018), *Review of Educational Research* 88(4). Covers 60 causal studies (mostly RCTs). Pooled effects: **0.49 SD on instruction** and **0.18 SD on achievement** [V]. Much of the evidence comes from literacy coaching for pre-K and elementary teachers. — [Brown Annenberg abstract](https://annenberg.brown.edu/publications/effect-teacher-coaching-instruction-and-achievement-meta-analysis-causal-evidence); [PDF](https://education.umd.edu/sites/default/files/uploads/inline-files/Kraft%20Blazar%20Hogan_2018_The%20Effect%20of%20Teacher%20Coaching_Review%20of%20Educational%20Research.pdf)
- **Scale-up attenuation.** Effectiveness trials of large programmes show "only a fraction" of the effects found in efficacy trials of small programmes. Small programmes (≤50 teachers) had about 2x the instruction effect and about 3x the achievement effect of programmes with ≥100 teachers [V, from the Learning Forward summary]. — [Learning Forward summary](https://learningforward.org/journal/august-2018-vol-39-no-4/the-impact-of-coaching-on-teacher-practice-and-student-achievement/)
- **Version discrepancy.** An earlier 2017 working-paper summary reported a 37-study base and different sub-estimates (coaching plus group training: 0.31 SD instruction, 0.12 SD achievement). Quote only the 2018 published figures. — [Learning Forward 2017 PDF](https://learningforward.org/wp-content/uploads/2017/08/meta-analysis-reveals-coachings-positive-impact-on-instruction-and-achievement.pdf)
- **Lesson study (EEF RCT, England, about 181 schools).** No effect on pupil attainment on average or for any subgroup [V]. Defenders argue the trial tested a "first-step" version and that control schools already used similar practices. — [Durrington Research School summary](https://researchschool.org.uk/durrington/news/eef-evaluations-lesson-study-and-teacher-observation)
- **Structured teacher observation / peer observation (EEF RCT, 82 schools).** No impact on GCSE English or maths [V]. — [EEF news](https://educationendowmentfoundation.org.uk/news/increasing-structured-teacher-observations-makes-no-difference)
- **Collective teacher efficacy (CTE).**
  - Hattie's d = 1.57 comes from one 2011 dissertation (Eells): a meta-analysis of 26 correlational studies, with r = 0.617 converted to d [V].
  - Corwin/Visible Learning have since revised the figure to 1.49, and then 1.39 [V].
  - Critics say the r-to-d conversion inflates the number and that causation is unidentified (high-achieving schools may produce high efficacy beliefs, not the reverse). Those critiques were found second-hand. — [Corwin FAQ](https://corwinvisiblelearningsolutions.sagepub.com/support/solutions/articles/7000090507-what-is-the-evidence-for-the-1-57-effect-size-for-collective-teacher-efficacy-); [visible-learning.org](https://visible-learning.org/2018/03/collective-teacher-efficacy-hattie/)
- **LMIC context.** The Global Education Evidence Advisory Panel (GEEAP, 2023, World Bank/FCDO/UNICEF) rates as "Great Buys": (a) structured pedagogy, meaning teacher guides plus training and coaching linked to materials, and (b) teaching at the right level. Generic teacher professional development is not among the Great Buys. — [World Bank Smart Buys page](https://www.worldbank.org/en/topic/teachingandlearning/publication/cost-effective-approaches-to-improve-global-learning); [press release](https://www.worldbank.org/en/news/press-release/2023/05/09/education-smart-buys-cost-effectively-supporting-teachers-and-parents-can-lead-to-significant-learning-improvements)

### Inferences
- Coaching is the best-evidenced teacher-development lever, but its power depends on intensity and coach quality, and those dilute at scale. A head-teacher "peer coaching" MIP is a weak copy of what was studied (dedicated, trained coaches, usually tied to a specific curriculum).
  - Realistic expectation for a 2–8 week head-teacher version: small or uncertain gains.
  - It works better when attached to a specific content routine, such as a structured-pedagogy or foundational literacy lesson plan, than as generic feedback.
- Lesson study, peer observation and PLCs are cheap and HT/SMC-feasible. However, the rigorous trials are null, so as learning interventions they should be ranked low. Position them as process or culture projects.
- CTE should not appear in an "effect-size ranked" list. At most it can be a framing concept.
- MIP-feasible? Lesson study, PLCs and peer observation: HT (low cost; null rigorous evidence). Coaching as studied: SYSTEM (needs coaches; the cluster resource coordinator (CRC/BRC) structure could host it).

### Gaps
- I found no rigorous meta-analysis of PLCs' causal effect on achievement this session. The PLC literature is mostly correlational (not verified this session).
- Indian RCTs of coaching or mentoring (for example, state FLN mentoring under NIPUN Bharat) were not checked this session.

## 2. School leadership and management

### Takeaway
Management quality correlates strongly with outcomes across countries, and India scores low. Still, the flagship Indian RCT of school self-assessment plus a school improvement plan found **zero learning effect** at scale, and the Campbell review finds school-based management works mainly in middle-income settings, not low-income or low-parental-education ones. This is directly relevant: the MIP catalogue is about 60% head-teacher-owned management projects.

### Cited Findings
- **Bloom, Lemos, Sadun & Van Reenen (2015), "Does Management Matter in Schools?",** *Economic Journal* 125(584):647–674.
  - Management survey of over 1,800 high schools in 8 countries.
  - "Higher management quality is strongly associated with better educational outcomes." This is **associational, not causal** [V].
  - The UK, Sweden, Canada and the US score highest. Italy, Brazil and **India** score lowest [V].
  - About half of the gap between autonomous and regular government schools is explained by principal leadership and governance [V]. — [NBER w20667](https://www.nber.org/papers/w20667); [World Management Survey](https://worldmanagementsurvey.org/?p=17479)
- **Muralidharan & Singh (2020; RISE WP 20/056), the MP School Quality Assurance (MPSQA) RCT, Madhya Pradesh.**
  - Programme: school assessment and rating, a school-specific improvement plan, and supervisor follow-up.
  - Sample: 51 treatment clusters (1,774 schools) vs 102 control clusters (3,661 schools). Assessment completion was 93%.
  - **No impact on learning** at 3–4 months or at 15–18 months, on both school-administered and independent tests [V].
  - It did not change management or teaching practices, and supervisors did not raise support or monitoring.
  - Qualitative work suggests officials treated it as a data-collection exercise.
  - The non-experimental Phase II evaluation of the national Shaala Siddhi variant (in over 600,000 schools) also found no learning impact [V]. — [J-PAL summary](https://www.povertyactionlab.org/node/4841173); [RISE WP PDF](https://riseprogramme.org/sites/default/files/2020-11/RISE_WP-056_Muralidharan_Singh.pdf); [VoxDev](https://voxdev.org/topic/education/improving-public-sector-management-scale)
- **Carr-Hill, Rolleston & Schendel (2016), Campbell Systematic Review 12(1), school-based decision-making in LMICs.**
  - 26 impact studies of 17 interventions (search up to January 2015).
  - Small-to-moderate positive effects on test scores, dropout and repetition, **mainly in middle-income countries**. No significant effect in low-income countries [V].
  - Benefits are smaller where parents have low education or low status relative to school staff [V].
  - Test scores were the most robust outcome. — [3ie record](https://3ieimpact.org/node/142); [full text, UCL](https://discovery.ucl.ac.uk/10092387/1/CL2.127.pdf); [Evidence Aid summary](https://www.evidenceaid.org/school%e2%80%90based-decision%e2%80%90making-in-low%e2%80%90and-middle%e2%80%90income-countries)
- **Indonesia school committee RCT (Pradhan et al., AEJ: Applied 2014).**
  - Grants plus training for existing school committees had limited learning impact.
  - Interventions **linking** the school committee to the village council were more effective [V, from the GSDRC summary]. Specific SD figures were not verified this session (my recollection is about 0.17–0.22 SD in Indonesian for the linkage arm) [UNVERIFIED]. — [GSDRC summary](https://gsdrc.org/document-library/improving-educational-quality-through-enhancing-community-participation-results-from-a-randomised-field-experiment-in-indonesia/)

### Inferences
- "Make a school development plan / self-assess / fill a format" type MIPs carry the strongest *negative* rigorous evidence in India. The Indian evidence says plan-and-report exercises do not move learning unless they change what happens in classrooms.
- Management MIPs should be re-specified around classroom-facing routines with a clear causal link to instruction, such as:
  - regrouping by level,
  - tracking teacher attendance or time-on-task,
  - weekly use of learning data to regroup children.
- SMC empowerment works best when it connects the SMC to local power holders (the gram panchayat) rather than only training it (Indonesia).
- MIP-feasible? Self-assessment and SDP: HT (but zero learning evidence). Management capacity programmes as in Bloom et al.: SYSTEM.

### Gaps
- The Snilstveit et al. (2016, 3ie Systematic Review 24) estimates for school-based management were not fetched this session. My recollection is that SBM showed small or insignificant learning effects in that review [UNVERIFIED].
- World Bank school-leadership trials (for example, Fryer's principal training in Houston; Lassibille/Madagascar AGEPA; Romero et al. on leadership training in Mexico) were not verified this session.

## 3. Parent and family engagement

### Takeaway
High-frequency, school-to-parent information on a child's attendance and grades is the best-evidenced, lowest-cost family lever. It cuts course failures and absences, with some learning gains (about 0.09–0.11 SD maths in Chile), and effects are larger for at-risk students. It is easily run by a head teacher using WhatsApp or SMS.

### Cited Findings
- **Bergman & Chan (2021), *J. Human Resources* 56(1):125–158, US RCT (West Virginia district).**
  - Design: automated weekly texts to parents on absences per class, missed assignments and low grades.
  - Results: course failures **−27%**, class attendance **+12%**, **no effect on state test scores** [V].
  - Effects were concentrated in high school and below-median-GPA students. No significant effect in middle school.
  - Cost: about **$63 in variable cost for over 32,000 texts** [V]. — [EconPapers abstract](https://econpapers.repec.org/RePEc:uwp:jhriss:v:56:y:2021:i:1:p:125-158); [JHR](https://jhr.uwpress.org/content/56/1/125)
- **Berlinski, Busso, Dinkelman & Martínez, "Papás al Día" (Chile, 2014–15; JHR 2025, 60(4):1284–1322).**
  - Design: RCT with about 1,000 children in 7 low-income schools; weekly and monthly SMS on attendance, grades and behaviour.
  - About 1 in 4 parents could not correctly report their child's grades or attendance at baseline.
  - Results: positive effects on grades and attendance. **Maths +0.09 SD** (working paper) vs **0.11 SD** (J-PAL summary); the discrepancy reflects different versions [V].
  - No average effect on behaviour notes. Effects doubled for students 1 SD higher on the at-risk index. Classroom spillovers were positive. — [NBER w28581](https://www.nber.org/system/files/working_papers/w28581/w28581.pdf); [J-PAL](https://www.povertyactionlab.org/print/pdf/node/2063?lang=en); [JHR](https://jhr.uwpress.org/node/15273)
- **York, Loeb & Doss (2019), READY4K!, *JHR* 54(3):537–566.**
  - Design: 8-month literacy-tip texting programme for parents of **preschoolers** (San Francisco).
  - Results: parental involvement +0.15 to 0.29 SD; child early literacy **about +0.11 SD** [V].
  - Note: this is a pre-K finding, outside the primary-high school scope, but it is a model for home-learning nudges. — [NBER w20659](https://www.nber.org/papers/W20659); [Brown Annenberg](https://annenberg.brown.edu/publications/one-step-time-effects-early-literacy-text-messaging-program-parents-preschoolers)
- **GEEAP 2023** lists "giving information on the benefits, costs and quality of education" and supporting parents among cost-effective approaches (the press release headline is "supporting teachers and parents"). — [World Bank press release](https://www.worldbank.org/en/news/press-release/2023/05/09/education-smart-buys-cost-effectively-supporting-teachers-and-parents-can-lead-to-significant-learning-improvements)

### Inferences
- A head-teacher MIP "weekly WhatsApp/SMS to parents on their child's absences and test results" is directly analogous to Bergman & Chan and Berlinski et al. It is:
  - very low cost,
  - deliverable in 2–8 weeks,
  - non-controversial.
- Expect attendance and grade or course-failure gains first. Expect test-score gains to be small (≤0.1 SD) or absent.
- Caution: LMIC trials used reliable, personalised data on each child. The MIP would need teachers to record attendance and assessment data per child. Generic broadcast messages are a different, weaker intervention.
- MIP-feasible? HT/SMC (yes).

### Gaps
- Parent-involvement meta-analyses (for example, Jeynes; See & Gorard 2015 for EEF; EEF "Parental engagement" toolkit, about +4 months) were not verified this session. Treat any figure as [UNVERIFIED].
- Indian RCTs of parent SMS or WhatsApp nudges (for example, COVID-era phone-call or SMS trials such as Angrist, Bergman & Matsheng in Botswana, about 0.12 SD for SMS plus phone calls) were not verified this session [UNVERIFIED].
- Home-learning-support evidence specific to India was not found this session.

## 4. Attendance and dropout

### Takeaway
- Cheap, school-run options with RCT support: early-warning data systems (−4 pp chronic absence) and information on returns (+0.20 years of schooling).
- Mentoring such as Check & Connect is effective but staff-intensive.
- Cash transfers raise enrolment and attendance but need system money and barely move test scores.
- India's midday meal shows sizeable learning associations (quasi-experimental).
- Deworming is **controversial**.

### Cited Findings
- **Early Warning Intervention and Monitoring System (EWIMS), Faria et al. 2017 (IES/REL Midwest).**
  - Design: cluster RCT, 73 high schools in 3 US states, 37,671 students in grades 9–10.
  - Results: chronic absence **10% vs 14%** (−4 pp); failed ≥1 course **21% vs 26%** (−5 pp) [V].
  - No effect on suspensions, GPA or credits. These are first-year results, achieved despite limited implementation. — [IES report](https://ies.ed.gov/use-work/resource-library/report/impact-study/getting-students-track-graduation-impacts-early-warning-intervention-and-monitoring-system-after-one); [REL infographic](https://ies.ed.gov/sites/default/files/migrated/rel/infographics/pdf/REL_MW_Early_Warning_Intervention_and_Monitoring_System.pdf)
- **Check & Connect (mentor or monitor model, US).**
  - WWC rating: "positive effects" for staying in school, with an improvement index of +25 percentile points (range +18 to +31), and "potentially positive" for progressing in school [V].
  - The evidence base is small (few studies).
  - Social Programs That Work rates it "Suggestive". — [WWC summary PDF](https://ed-psych.utah.edu/school-psych/_resources/documents/grants/autism-training-grant/CC-WWC.pdf); [Social Programs That Work](https://evidencebasedprograms.org/document/check-and-connect-evidence-summary)
- **Jensen (2010), *QJE* 125(2):515–548, Dominican Republic.**
  - Grade-8 boys underestimated returns to secondary school.
  - Telling randomly selected schools the measured returns led to **0.20–0.35 more years of schooling** over 4 years [V]. — [EconPapers](https://econpapers.repec.org/RePEc:oup:qjecon:v:125:y:2010:i:2:p:515-548.); [J-PAL](https://www.povertyactionlab.org/print/pdf/node/1727?lang=en)
- **Cash transfers: Baird, Ferreira, Özler & Woolcock (2014),** *J. Development Effectiveness* 6(1), 35 studies.
  - Both CCTs and UCTs raise enrolment and attendance odds.
  - Enforced, monitored conditions show the largest effects, about **60% higher odds of enrolment**, vs **18–25%** for no or unmonitored conditions (these odds figures are from the summary of the 2013 Campbell version).
  - The CCT–UCT difference is not statistically significant.
  - Test-score effects are "small at best" [V]. — [World Bank OKR](https://openknowledge.worldbank.org/entities/publication/c7a280db-e64f-5fd7-85ae-bde03e7625ea/full); [GSDRC](https://gsdrc.org/document-library/relative-effectiveness-of-conditional-and-unconditional-cash-transfers-for-schooling-outcomes-in-developing-countries-a-systematic-review/)
- **India midday meal: Chakraborty & Jayaraman (2019),** *J. Development Economics*.
  - Design: quasi-experimental, using the staggered state rollout after the 2001 Supreme Court order. Data: about 600 rural districts, over 200,000 households.
  - Results: full primary exposure gives **reading +0.17 SD (18%)** and **maths +0.09 SD (9%)**, with diminishing returns to exposure [V].
  - The authors flag possible upward bias from cohort and state confounds. — [IZA DP 10086](https://docs.iza.org/dp10086.pdf); [VoxDev](https://voxdev.org/topic/education/midday-meals-india-improve-childrens-learning-outcomes)
- **Deworming: CONTROVERSIAL.**
  - Original study: Miguel & Kremer (2004, *Econometrica*), Kenya; school absenteeism down about a quarter [UNVERIFIED this session for the exact figure].
  - The 3ie-funded replication (Aiken, Davey, Hargreaves & Hayes 2015, LSHTM) reproduced most results but questioned the education benefits, citing no exam benefit and large missing data [V].
  - The 2015 Cochrane review (Taylor-Robinson et al.; 45 trials) found no evidence that mass deworming affects weight, height, haemoglobin, **exam performance** or mortality [V].
  - Evidence Action, Miguel and Kremer dispute the reanalysis ("worm wars"). — [World Bank "Worm Wars" anthology](https://blogs.worldbank.org/impactevaluations/worm-wars-anthology); [3ie replication](https://3ieimpact.org/node/145); [Cochrane editorial](https://cochranelibrary.com/cdsr/doi/10.1002/14651858.ED000116/full); [Evidence Action response](https://www.evidenceaction.org/insights/worms-win-kids-lose-our-statement)

### Inferences
- **Best MIP fits:**
  - (a) A simple early-warning register: the head teacher flags students with low attendance, low marks or behaviour issues each week and assigns a teacher-mentor to follow up. This is EWIMS-lite combined with Check & Connect-lite.
  - (b) Parent attendance alerts (Section 3).
  - (c) A "returns to schooling" information session for grades 7–10 students and parents, modelled on Jensen. This is very cheap.
- Cash transfers and meals are SYSTEM-level; the midday meal already exists. A head teacher can, however, improve **meal regularity and quality** within the existing scheme. There is no direct RCT on that margin.
- Deworming: India already runs National Deworming Day. A school MIP could support uptake for health reasons, but it should **not** be ranked on learning or attendance claims, given the controversy.
- MIP-feasible?

| Intervention | Who can run it |
|---|---|
| Early-warning register | HT |
| Mentoring | HT (labour-heavy) |
| Jensen-style information | HT/SMC |
| Cash transfers | SYSTEM |
| Meals | SYSTEM (implementation quality is HT) |
| Deworming | SYSTEM (health dept.) |

### Gaps
- Indian RCTs of early-warning systems or dropout mentoring were not found this session.
- A Jensen-style information replication in India (for example, Nguyen 2008 in Madagascar showed test-score gains) was not verified this session [UNVERIFIED].
- Muralidharan & Prakash's (2017) bicycle-for-girls programme in Bihar (+32% enrolment in secondary, quasi-experimental) was not verified this session [UNVERIFIED]. It is SYSTEM-level.

## 5. Student voice / participation in school governance / participatory budgeting

### Takeaway
I found **no rigorous (RCT or quasi-experimental) evidence that student councils, student voice or school participatory budgeting improve learning outcomes.** The evidence I found is descriptive, or reports civic outcomes from non-randomised designs.

### Cited Findings
- The closest rigorous adjacent evidence concerns adult school committees, not students: in the Indonesia RCT, committee–village linkage beat training and grants (see Section 2). — [GSDRC](https://gsdrc.org/document-library/improving-educational-quality-through-enhancing-community-participation-results-from-a-randomised-field-experiment-in-indonesia/)
- The Hudson High School (Massachusetts) democratic-governance initiative has a five-year non-randomised comparison evaluation. Outcomes are civic knowledge and participation, not learning. — [CIRCLE WP70](https://circle.Tufts.Edu/sites/default/files/2019-12/WP70_FiveYearEvaluationofaComprehensiveHighSchoolCivicEngagementInitiative_2010.pdf)
- The remaining sources (a Kenyan student-council thesis, Polish "School Democracy" reports) are descriptive. — [EEA Grants School Democracy Laboratory](https://eeagrants.org/node/20075)

### Inferences
- Student-council or bal sansad MIPs can be retained for civic or wellbeing reasons, but should be labelled "no learning evidence". They are cheap and non-controversial, but should not be ranked by effect size.

### Gaps
- No RCT of school participatory budgeting on any outcome was found.

## 6. Grouping and class size

### Takeaway
Regrouping children by **current learning level** has strong LMIC RCT support:
- Kenya tracking: +0.14 SD at 18 months, persisting.
- Teaching at the right level (TaRL), India: classed as a "Great Buy".

It is low-cost and HT-runnable. By contrast, EEF evidence on rich-country setting or streaming is negative or neutral for low attainers. Class-size reduction works only at deep cuts and is very expensive.

### Cited Findings
- **Duflo, Dupas & Kremer (2011), *AER* 101(5):1739–74, Kenya, 121 schools [count UNVERIFIED].**
  - Grade-1 classes split by initial achievement.
  - After 18 months, tracking schools scored **+0.14 SD** (2008 working-paper abstract).
  - The AER text reports **+0.16 SD one year after tracking ended**, i.e. the effect persisted [V].
  - Both high and low initial achievers benefited. Teachers tailored instruction to their group's level. — [EconPapers AER](https://econpapers.repec.org/RePEc:aea:aecrev:v:101:y:2011:i:5:p:1739-74); [SSRN](https://papers.ssrn.com/author=1311167)
- **Teaching at the Right Level** (grouping by level, not grade) is a **GEEAP 2023 "Great Buy"** [V]. — [World Bank Smart Buys](https://www.worldbank.org/en/topic/teachingandlearning/publication/cost-effective-approaches-to-improve-global-learning)
- **EEF setting/streaming:** not retrieved this session (the search failed). My recollection is that the toolkit gives "setting or streaming" about −1 month on average, with negative effects for low attainers, and "within-class attainment grouping" about +2 months [UNVERIFIED].
- **Class size (EEF toolkit):**
  - About **+2 months** progress (older versions: +3), on "very limited" evidence [V].
  - Effects appear only when classes fall below about 20 or even 15 pupils.
  - Cuts from 30 to 25 are "unlikely to be cost-effective".
  - Costs are very high (extra staff). — [EEF toolkit](https://educationendowmentfoundation.org.uk/education-evidence/teaching-learning-toolkit/reducing-class-size)
- **Tennessee STAR** (Krueger 1999, *QJE*): classes of 13–17 vs 22–25 gave about 0.2 SD in early grades [UNVERIFIED this session]. **Angrist & Lavy (1999, *QJE*), Israel**, used the Maimonides rule (RD design): gains in grades 4–5 [UNVERIFIED this session]. Neither was re-fetched.

### Inferences
- An MIP that "regroups children for 1 hour/day by current level in reading and maths using a quick oral assessment" maps onto the strongest LMIC evidence in this whole inventory.
  - It is HT-runnable, low-cost and fits 2–8 weeks (TaRL "camps" are typically 30–50 days).
  - It must be framed as **temporary, flexible level-grouping**, not fixed streaming, to stay non-controversial and avoid EEF-type harms to low attainers.
- Class-size reduction is SYSTEM-level (posts and teachers), and in India pupil–teacher ratios are often already the binding constraint. It is not a MIP.

### Gaps
- The EEF setting/streaming figures need verifying from the EEF site.
- The STAR and Angrist & Lavy effect sizes were not re-checked this session.

## 7. High-dosage tutoring and volunteer/peer tutoring in low-resource settings

### Takeaway
Tutoring is one of the largest, most robust effects in education:
- Meta-analysis of RCTs: **0.29 SD** published (0.37 SD in the working paper).
- In India, the Balsakhi community-tutor programme gave 0.14 SD (year 1) and 0.28 SD (year 2) school-wide, and about 0.6 SD for tutored children.

Volunteer or community-tutor models are low-cost and adaptable by a head teacher or SMC.

### Cited Findings
- **Nickow, Oreopoulos & Quan, tutoring meta-analysis of PreK–12 RCTs.**
  - Published version (*AERJ* 2024): **0.288 SD** pooled (SE 0.029; 89 RCTs); literacy 0.290, maths 0.268 [V].
  - NBER w27476 (2020) working paper: **0.37 SD**. Quote the published figure.
  - Tutoring **during the school day** has larger effects than after-school tutoring [V]. — [NBER w27476](https://www.nber.org/papers/w27476); [AERJ published](https://journals.sagepub.com/doi/10.3102/00028312231208687); [Stanford NSSA](https://nssa.stanford.edu/studies/promise-tutoring-prek-12-learning-systematic-review-and-metaanalysis-experimental-evidence)
- **Balsakhi (Banerjee, Cole, Duflo & Linden 2007, *QJE* 122(3)), Vadodara and Mumbai, India.**
  - Design: young local women tutors pull out lagging grade 3–4 children for remediation.
  - Results: school-level **+0.14 SD (year 1)** and **+0.28 SD (year 2)**; IV estimate for tutored children **about 0.6 SD**. Gains faded after the programme ended, with some persistence [V].
  - Cost: J-PAL correction, **$0.67 per 0.1 SD** (not per 1 SD) [V]. — [J-PAL](https://www.povertyactionlab.org/print/pdf/node/1415?lang=en); [paper PDF](https://files.givewell.org/files/DWDA%202009/IPA/Banerjee,%20Cole,%20Duflo,%20Linden%202006.pdf)

### Inferences
- A head-teacher or SMC MIP that recruits local volunteers (educated youth, older students, mothers' groups) to tutor lagging children in small groups **during school hours**, using a simple level-based routine, has strong and directly Indian evidence.
- The Balsakhi tutors were paid a small stipend. A purely unpaid volunteer model is less tested, so attendance and retention of volunteers is the main risk.
- Peer tutoring (older students tutoring younger ones) is cheaper still. LMIC RCT evidence was not verified this session.
- MIP-feasible? HT/SMC (yes, if volunteers are available). Paid high-dosage tutoring: SYSTEM money.

### Gaps
- LMIC peer-tutoring RCTs and the EEF peer-tutoring toolkit figure (about +5–6 months) were not verified this session [UNVERIFIED].
- The Pratham Read India / TaRL camp effect sizes (for example, Banerjee et al. 2016/2017, about 0.6–0.7 SD in Uttar Pradesh camps) were not re-checked this session [UNVERIFIED]. They should be confirmed before quoting.

## Cross-cutting ranking summary (for the report writer)

Ordered by rigour × effect size × MIP-fit. Figures are as verified above.

| # | Intervention | Best figure | Design / location | MIP-feasible? |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| 1 | Level-based regrouping / TaRL-style remediation | +0.14–0.16 SD | Kenya RCT; GEEAP Great Buy | HT, 2–8 wk |
| 2 | Community/volunteer tutoring in school hours | +0.14 → 0.28 SD school-level; 0.29 SD meta | Balsakhi RCT, India; meta of RCTs | HT/SMC |
| 3 | Parent SMS/WhatsApp on child-specific attendance and grades | −27% course failures; +12% attendance; +0.09–0.11 SD maths (Chile) | US + Chile RCTs | HT, very low cost |
| 4 | Early-warning register + mentor follow-up | −4 pp chronic absence; −5 pp course failure | US RCT | HT |
| 5 | Returns-to-schooling information session | +0.20–0.35 years schooling | Dominican Republic RCT | HT/SMC |
| 6 | Structured, content-linked coaching | 0.18 SD (meta), attenuates at scale | Mostly US RCTs | SYSTEM-supported; weak HT version |

**Low or no learning evidence** (keep only for non-learning reasons):
- School self-assessment / development plans: **null RCT in India**.
- Lesson study and peer observation: null EEF RCTs.
- PLCs: correlational evidence.
- Collective teacher efficacy: inflated correlational claim.
- Student councils and participatory budgeting: no learning evidence.

**SYSTEM-level:** cash transfers, class-size reduction, midday meal (exists), deworming (**controversial** for education claims).
