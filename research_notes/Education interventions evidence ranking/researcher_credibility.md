## 1. Credibility profiles of key researchers and organisations

### Takeaway
The list mixes three kinds of people:
- **Causal-evidence producers in LMIC education.** Banerjee, Duflo, Kremer, Muralidharan, Banerji/Pratham, Piper and Angrist run large RCTs, often in India or Africa.
- **Learning-science and synthesis figures.** Hattie, Wiliam, Rosenshine, Dunlosky, Roediger, Slavin and Kraft. Their reputations rest on lab experiments, meta-analysis or review work, mostly from high-income countries.
- **Adjacent-field researchers.** Ito, Pinkard, Goel, Tambe and Yeager. They are highly credentialed, but in anthropology, the learning sciences, computer science or social psychology. Their flagship frameworks (Connected Learning, participatory budgeting, AI for social good) have **little or no RCT evidence on K-12 learning outcomes in LMIC government schools**.

Reputation is high across the whole list. It does not track how strong the evidence is for the specific intervention being ranked.

**Robert E. Slavin (Johns Hopkins; died 2021)**

### Inferences
- For Indian government schools, the most directly relevant credibility belongs to a group of researchers who ran RCTs inside Indian government systems at scale: Banerjee, Duflo, Banerji/Pratham and Muralidharan. Second come those with LMIC-at-scale evidence: Piper, Angrist and Kremer. Nobel status adds little information beyond that track record.
- Learning-science names (Dunlosky, Roediger, Rosenshine, Wiliam) are credible on cognitive mechanisms. Their evidence is mostly from high-income countries, so they should get a context-relevance discount rather than a reputation discount.
- Ito, Pinkard, Goel and Tambe are reputable in their fields, but their frameworks should be scored as "promising, low causal evidence for K-12 learning outcomes." Reputation must not lift them.
- Several prominent figures have developer or commercial interests in the interventions they rate: Hattie (Visible Learning consultancy), Slavin (Success for All), Piper (Tusome), Pratham (TaRL). Whether an independent evaluation exists is a more useful flag than reputation.

## 2. Why reputation and citation counts are weak proxies for causal evidence, plus accepted evidence hierarchies

### Takeaway
Education research has very low replication rates, inflated effects in small or developer-run studies, and large gaps between meta-analytic averages and effects in large preregistered RCTs. Citations measure attention, not validity. The accepted hierarchies (EEF, WWC, GRADE, 3ie/GEEAP) all rate **the study design and the body of evidence, not the author**.

### Cited Findings
- **Makel and Plucker (2014),** "Facts Are More Important Than Novelty," *Educational Researcher*:
  - Only **0.13%** of articles in the top 100 education journals were replications (221 of 164,589).
  - About **67.4%** of replications reported success.
  - Psychology's rate is about 1.07%.
  - Sources: [AERA](https://www.aera.net/Newsroom/Recent-AERA-Research/Facts-Are-More-Important-Than-Novelty-Replication-in-the-Education-Sciences); [Inside Higher Ed](https://www.insidehighered.com/news/2014/08/14/almost-no-education-research-replicated-new-article-shows)
  - Critics note the keyword method ("replicat*") may undercount replications — [Hechinger Report](https://hechingerreport.org/education-researchers-dont-check-errors-dearth-replication-studies/)
  - Not verified this session: the commonly reported finding that success rates were lower when no original authors were on the replication team.
- **Lortie-Forgues and Inglis (2019),** *Educational Researcher*:
  - 141 large K-12 RCTs (EEF and NCEE), about 1.2 million students.
  - Mean effect **0.06 SD**, with wide confidence intervals.
  - Median Bayes factor 0.56, so many trials were uninformative. Their blog puts the share at about a third to a half of trials.
  - The "typical" literature effect is often quoted as 0.25–0.50 SD.
  - Sources: [White Rose](https://eprints.whiterose.ac.uk/141754/); [Loughborough blog](https://blog.lboro.ac.uk/cmc/2021/02/16/what-makes-education-trials-uninformative/)
  - Counter-view (Simpson): the uninformativeness verdict depends on a narrow fixed prior — [Durham](https://durham-repository.worktribe.com/OutputFile/1296385)
  - Counter-view (UCL): the fix is fewer, larger trials — [UCL comment](https://discovery.ucl.ac.uk/id/eprint/10101577/1/Informing%20better%20trial%20design%20-%20A%20Technical%20Comment%20on%20Lortie-Forgues%20and%20Inglis%202019.pdf)
- **Kraft (2020):** Cohen's 0.2/0.5/0.8 benchmarks were not derived from education and are too lenient. Effects that are "small" by Cohen's scale are large relative to typical field interventions — [CGD](https://www.cgdev.org/node/3128013); [Annenberg](https://annenberg.brown.edu/publications/interpreting-effect-sizes-education-interventions)
- **Simpson's critique of effect-size comparisons:** effect sizes depend on sample, comparison condition and outcome measure. That undermines league tables that compare effect sizes across studies, including Kraft's benchmarks and Hattie's rankings — [Durham repository](https://durham-repository.worktribe.com/OutputFile/1284413)
- **Cheung and Slavin (2016):** small samples, researcher-made measures and quasi-experimental designs inflate education effect sizes — [bg DOI](https://doi.org/10.3102/0013189X16656615)
- **Psychology replication context:** the Open Science Collaboration (2015, *Science*) replicated about 36% of 100 psychology findings with significant results — [bg DOI](https://doi.org/10.1126/science.aac4716)
- **EEF padlocks** rate the strength of the evidence body (quantity, recency, share of RCTs, quality), separately from impact — [EEF Toolkit Guide 2025](https://d2tic4wvo1iusb.cloudfront.net/production/documents/toolkit/toolkit_guide_2025_v3.0.0.pdf)
- **GRADE:**
  - Rates certainty as high, moderate, low or very low.
  - RCT bodies of evidence start "high" and are downgraded for risk of bias, inconsistency, indirectness, imprecision and publication bias.
  - Author reputation is not a criterion.
  - [GRADE Working Group [bg]](https://www.gradeworkinggroup.org/)
  - The "indirectness" criterion maps naturally onto "evidence from the UK/US applied to Indian government schools."
- **3ie / GEEAP:** LMIC-specific synthesis and cost-effectiveness tiers. The GEEAP tiers are Great / Good / Promising-but-limited / Bad Buys — [3ie SR7](https://3ieimpact.org/SRS7); [World Bank](https://www.worldbank.org/en/topic/teachingandlearning/publication/cost-effective-approaches-to-improve-global-learning)
- **UK What Works Network:** EEF is the education member [bg](https://www.gov.uk/guidance/what-works-network)

### Inferences
- Reputation tells you about **expected average study quality**. Prestigious researchers usually preregister, use large samples and publish nulls. It is never a substitute for checking the specific study's design.
- The most defensible use of reputation is to resolve close calls and to flag where independent verification is needed. Lifting a score is not defensible.
- A gap between the meta-analytic estimate (for example the EEF Toolkit "+6 months") and effects in large, independent, preregistered trials (about 0.06 SD on average) should be scored explicitly. Large trials should be weighted more heavily.

### Gaps
- No formal study was found that measures how well researcher prestige or h-index predicts replication in education specifically. Psychology has some work on this, but it was not retrieved here.
- The exact EEF formula converting months to effect size was not confirmed from the EEF documents. The figure of about 0.1 SD per month quoted by some blogs is unverified.

## 3. Documented controversies and replication problems

### Takeaway
Every high-profile "brand" on the list has a live methodological debate. The debates mostly concern effect-size inflation, generalisability or reanalysis, **not misconduct**. No retractions of these researchers' education papers were found, but no systematic Retraction Watch search was run.

### Cited Findings

**Hattie's Visible Learning methodology**
- Criticisms:
  - It treats Cohen's d as a universal, comparable metric across non-equivalent study types.
  - It pools meta-analyses without screening the quality of the underlying studies.
  - It contains Common Language Effect Size calculation errors, which Hattie acknowledged.
  - Its estimates move between editions (teacher-student relationships went from 0.72 to 0.52).
  - It treats 0.40 as a "hinge point."
  - Its meta-meta-analytic formula is opaque.
  - [Bergeron & Rivard, McGill Journal of Education](https://mje.mcgill.ca/article/download/9475/7229?inline=1); [MDPI Education Sciences 2018](https://mdpi.com/2227-7102/8/4/174); [UBC Critical Education](https://ices.library.ubc.ca/index.php/criticaled/article/view/186745/185678); [Didau summary](https://daviddidau.substack.com/p/visible-learning-invisible-errors)
- Slavin, "John Hattie is Wrong" (blog, June 2018) and his Education Week letter (July 2018): "a meta-analysis cannot be better than the studies it contains" — [Education Week](https://www.edweek.org/leadership/opinion-john-hattie-is-still-wrong/2018/07)
- Defences:
  - DeWitt argues Hattie is being misused — [Education Week](https://www.edweek.org/leadership/opinion-john-hattie-isnt-wrong-you-are-misusing-his-research/2018/06)
  - Gene Glass, who originated meta-analysis, called the Slavin criticism inappropriate — [NEPC newsletter](https://nepc.colorado.edu/sites/default/files/publications/Newsletter%20glass_0.pdf)
  - The 2023 sequel warns against treating effect sizes as absolute — [Didau](https://daviddidau.substack.com/p/visible-learning-invisible-errors)
- Source quality: the McGill piece is an opinion and critique article, and several sources are blogs.

**Deworming ("worm wars")**
- In 2015 the *International Journal of Epidemiology* published two reanalyses of Miguel and Kremer (2004):
  - Aiken et al. found a coding error. The externality calculation counted only the nearest 12 schools, and once corrected, cross-school effects at 3–6 km were no longer significant.
  - Davey et al. took an epidemiological approach.
  - [GiveWell](https://blog.givewell.org/2015/07/24/new-deworming-reanalyses-and-cochrane-review/); [The Conversation](https://theconversation.com/how-re-analysing-the-data-of-scientific-research-can-change-the-findings-44926)
- Hicks, Kremer and Miguel replied that the reanalysis still supports the externality and school-participation effects. J-PAL states the authors stand by the findings — [J-PAL](https://www.povertyactionlab.org/updates/deworming-informed-debate-requires-careful-look-data?lang=fr)
- Ozier (2019, World Bank WP) uses the case to ask whether economics has a reproducibility crisis — [GiveWell](https://blog.givewell.org/2015/07/24/new-deworming-reanalyses-and-cochrane-review/)
- The Cochrane reviews (Taylor-Robinson et al. 2015/2019) found little evidence that mass deworming improves school performance — [bg](https://www.cochranelibrary.com/cdsr/doi/10.1002/14651858.CD000371.pub7/full)
- Inference: this is a model case of a reputable lab's flagship result being partly corrected by replication with shared data and code. The open data itself is a credibility positive.

**Growth mindset**
- Sisk et al. (2018, *Psychological Science*) ran two meta-analyses:
  - Mindset vs achievement: 273 effect sizes, about 365,915 participants. The correlation was weak.
  - Interventions: 43 studies, about 57,155 participants. Effects were weak, with an overall d reported as about 0.08 (verify against the paper).
  - [Sisk et al. PDF](https://englelab.gatech.edu/articles/2018/Sisk,%20Burgoyne%20et%20al.%20(2018)%20-%20Mindset%20and%20Academic%20Achievement.pdf); [MSU Today](https://msutoday.msu.edu/news/2018/05/study-finds-popular-growth-mindset-educational-interventions-arent-very-effective); [EdSurge](https://edsurge.com/news/2018-05-29-believe-and-you-can-achieve-researchers-find-limited-gains-from-growth-mindset-interventions)
- Macnamara and Burgoyne (2022, *Psychological Bulletin*) found d = 0.05 across 63 studies (N = 97,672) and **d = 0.02 in the six highest-quality studies** (N = 13,571). Burnette et al. (2022/23) reported positive effects for some subgroups. The two teams disagree on how to treat heterogeneity — [Structural Learning summary](https://www.structural-learning.com/post/growth-mindset-what-research-actually-shows); [Durham repository](https://durham-repository.worktribe.com/OutputFile/3959161)
- Yeager and Dweck counter that critics use the wrong effect-size benchmark. By Kraft's scale, small but cheap and scalable effects can matter — [Progress Focused](https://www.progressfocused.com/2021/01/answers-to-four-critical-questions.html)

**SEL: Durlak (2011) vs newer work**
- Durlak et al. (2011): 213 universal school-based SEL programmes, 270,034 K-12 students, and an **11 percentile-point achievement gain** — [Social Thinking summary of Durlak 2011](https://www.socialthinking.com/evidence/peer-reviewed/impact-of-social-emotional-learning)
- Cipriano et al. (2023, *Child Development*): 424 studies (2008–2020), more than 50 countries, more than 500,000 students. Broadly positive results, including on grades and test scores — [Hechinger Report](https://hechingerreport.org/proof-points-a-research-update-on-social-emotional-learning-in-schools/); [KQED MindShift](https://www.kqed.org/mindshift/62194/is-social-emotional-learning-effective-new-meta-analysis-adds-to-evidence-but-debate-persists)
- Critiques:
  - Both meta-analyses were authored by SEL proponents.
  - Many included studies would not meet WWC or RAND standards.
  - Researcher-made outcome measures can inflate effects.
  - Max Eden (AEI) argues SEL is "oversold."
  - Cipriano says unpublished null studies were sought and the data is public.
  - [Hechinger Report](https://hechingerreport.org/proof-points-a-research-update-on-social-emotional-learning-in-schools/); [FutureEd](https://www.future-ed.org/parsing-the-evidence-on-social-emotional-learnings-impact/)
- I found no direct reanalysis of the 11-percentile figure.

**Direct Instruction and explicit-instruction debates**
- Project Follow Through (1968–1977), the large US comparison, is often cited as favouring Engelmann's Direct Instruction. Stockard et al. (2018, *Review of Educational Research*) meta-analysed half a century of DI research and reported moderate-to-large positive effects — [bg DOI](https://doi.org/10.3102/0034654317751919)
- Critics dispute Follow Through's design, which was not randomised and had site heterogeneity. Stockard et al. is from the National Institute for Direct Instruction, a developer-affiliated organisation [bg].
- The related "explicit vs minimally guided instruction" argument (Kirschner, Sweller and Clark 2006, *Educational Psychologist*) is partly ideological. Constructivist researchers dispute its framing [bg DOI](https://doi.org/10.1207/s15326985ep4102_1)
- Inference: the closest LMIC evidence (structured pedagogy, the GEEAP Great Buy) is a scripted or explicit-instruction cousin, and it has independent LMIC support.

**Learning styles**
- Pashler, McDaniel, Rohrer and Bjork (2008, *Psychological Science in the Public Interest*) found "virtually no evidence" for the meshing hypothesis, in which matching instruction to a preferred style improves learning. They concluded there is no adequate basis for using learning-style assessments in practice, while noting this is not conclusive disproof — [USF Scholar Commons](https://digitalcommons.usf.edu/psy_facpub/1765/); [Learning Guild](https://www.learningguild.com/articles/research-discredits-learning-styles-matching-hypothesis)
- Use this as a **red-flag exemplar**: an idea with enormous practitioner reputation and no causal support.

**India and LMIC-specific replication caveats**
- TaRL: several government-school implementation designs had no impact before two later models worked. This shows effects depend on how the programme is delivered — [NBER w22931](https://www.nber.org/papers/w22931)
- Mindspark: large effects in the Delhi after-school centres, but a null average effect from the in-school Rajasthan follow-up — [AEA Registry 2459](https://www.socialscienceregistry.org/trials/2459); [Education Next](https://www.educationnext.org/delhi-experiment-software-sparks-success/)
- Formative assessment: Kingston and Nash (2011) estimated about 0.20, against the 0.4–0.7 often cited from Black and Wiliam [bg](https://doi.org/10.1111/j.1745-3992.2011.00220.x)

### Inferences
- Controversy flags should be graded:
  - A (resolved, minor)
  - B (effect size contested but the direction holds, as with Hattie's specific numbers, growth mindset and SEL)
  - C (core claim not supported, as with learning styles)
- Deworming is not a pedagogy "transformer," but it is a useful calibration case for the scheme.
- Failures in the scale-up context (TaRL's early versions, Mindspark in school) matter more for Indian government schools than academic disputes do. Implementation-at-scale evidence should be its own criterion.

### Gaps
- No systematic Retraction Watch or PubPeer search was done for the named researchers. The finding of "no retractions" means none were found, not that none exist.
- The Davey et al. (2015) conclusions and the long-run deworming follow-ups (for example Hamory et al. 2021, *PNAS*) were not reviewed in detail.
- No independent LMIC RCTs of retrieval practice, peer tutoring, Connected Learning or school participatory budgeting were identified within this scope. Other researchers may cover them.

## Annex within scope: recommended scoring scheme (reputation as a confidence modifier only)

Each line below cites its basis. The weights are a proposal (my inference), not taken from any source.

| Dimension | Weight | How to score | Basis |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Evidence quality** | 35% | Use GRADE-style certainty (High 4 / Moderate 3 / Low 2 / Very low 1). Start from design (RCT > QED > correlational). Downgrade for small samples, researcher-made measures, high attrition, no preregistration, and developer-run evaluation with no independent replication. Upgrade for multiple independent replications. | [GRADE [bg]](https://www.gradeworkinggroup.org/); [EEF padlocks](https://d2tic4wvo1iusb.cloudfront.net/production/documents/toolkit/toolkit_guide_2025_v3.0.0.pdf); [WWC [bg]](https://ies.ed.gov/ncee/wwc/Handbooks); Cheung & Slavin 2016 [bg] |
| **Effect size** | 25% | Use the best-quality estimate, not the largest. Kraft bands: <0.05 small (1), 0.05–0.19 medium (2–3), ≥0.20 large (4). Prefer estimates from large or independent RCTs over meta-meta-analyses (Hattie). | [Kraft 2020](https://edworkingpapers.com/ai19-10); [Lortie-Forgues & Inglis](https://eprints.whiterose.ac.uk/141754/) |
| **Context relevance (LMIC/India, government schools, at scale)** | 20% | 4 = RCT in Indian government schools at scale. 3 = other LMIC at scale. 2 = LMIC pilot or NGO-run. 1 = high-income only or lab. This works like GRADE "indirectness." | [GEEAP 2023](https://www.worldbank.org/en/topic/teachingandlearning/publication/cost-effective-approaches-to-improve-global-learning); [NBER w22931](https://www.nber.org/papers/w22931) |
| **Cost and feasibility** | 15% | Use cost per 0.1 SD or the GEEAP tier, plus teacher-time and tech requirements in a micro-improvement project. | [Angrist et al. 2023 via World Bank](https://blogs.worldbank.org/en/education/focus-improving-learning-experience-classroom) |
| **Controversy flag** | Deduction 0 / −0.5 / −1.5 | A/B/C grading as in Section 3 | Section 3 |
| **Researcher/organisation credibility** | Not additive. A ±1-step confidence modifier and tie-breaker only. | Score: (a) track record of preregistered, open-data trials; (b) independence from the developer; (c) whether prior flagship results replicated. Use peer-reviewed output and position for orientation only. Citations and h-index are recorded but never scored. | Makel & Plucker 2014 ([AERA](https://www.aera.net/Newsroom/Recent-AERA-Research/Facts-Are-More-Important-Than-Novelty-Replication-in-the-Education-Sciences)); worm-wars case ([GiveWell](https://blog.givewell.org/2015/07/24/new-deworming-reanalyses-and-cochrane-review/)) |

Guard-rails (inference):
1. Reputation may only move a score within its evidence band. It cannot lift a "Very low" evidence item above "Low."
2. Any intervention whose evidence is produced only by its developer gets a "needs independent replication" flag, whatever the developer's prestige.
3. Record every citation count with an access date. Use them only to sanity-check that a researcher is established, never as a ranking input.
4. Report the evidence score and the reputation note in separate columns, so readers can see that reputation did not drive the rank.
