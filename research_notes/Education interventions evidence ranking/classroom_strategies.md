# Classroom Teaching and Learning Strategies: Experimental and Meta-Analytic Evidence (Primary, Middle, High School)

Notation and verification conventions:
- Effect sizes are standardized mean differences (d or Hedges' g, in SD units) unless stated. k = number of studies, N = participants.
- **[V]** = figure confirmed this session against the primary paper or a direct summary of it. **[UNVERIFIED]** = figure from the researcher's prior knowledge of the literature, linked to the original paper's DOI but NOT checked against the text this session. The report writer should treat [UNVERIFIED] figures as provisional and say so.
- Interpretation benchmark: Kraft (2020, *Educational Researcher*) argues that for causal studies of school interventions on standardized achievement, effects below 0.05 SD are small, 0.05 to under 0.20 medium, and 0.20 or more large; Hattie-style "0.40 hinge" benchmarks are inflated by lab studies, narrow researcher-made tests and short durations — [Kraft 2020, doi 10.3102/0013189X20912798](https://doi.org/10.3102/0013189X20912798) [UNVERIFIED thresholds]. This matters throughout: meta-analytic averages of 0.5 to 0.9 from short, researcher-designed tests shrink sharply on standardized tests in real classrooms.
- Session research budget limited verification; roughly half the figures below are [V].

## 1. Cognitive-science strategies (retrieval practice, spacing, interleaving, worked examples, elaboration, self-explanation)

### Takeaway
Retrieval practice (low-stakes quizzing) and spaced practice have the strongest and most replicated evidence, including in real classrooms, at near-zero cost; interleaving (especially in maths), worked examples and self-explanation have good but narrower evidence. Most of the base is high-income (US/UK/Europe) and secondary/undergraduate; primary-school classroom evidence on retrieval is thin.

### Cited Findings
- **Dunlosky et al. 2013 (Psychological Science in the Public Interest)** rated ten techniques. Only **practice testing** and **distributed practice** received "high utility"; summarization, highlighting/underlining and rereading were rated low — [APS press release](https://www.psychologicalscience.org/news/releases/which-study-strategies-make-the-grade.html) [V]. The middle ("moderate utility") tier was elaborative interrogation, self-explanation and interleaved practice; keyword mnemonic and imagery-for-text were low — [Dunlosky et al. 2013, doi 10.1177/1529100612453266](https://doi.org/10.1177/1529100612453266) [UNVERIFIED tier membership for moderate items]. Testing works even when massed but is more effective spaced — [APS](https://www.psychologicalscience.org/news/releases/which-study-strategies-make-the-grade.html) [V].
- **Adesope, Trevisan & Sundararajan 2017 (Review of Educational Research 87(3):659–701)**: 118 articles, 272 independent effect sizes, ~15,000 participants. Practice tests beat restudying and all other comparison conditions — [WSU repository](https://rex.libraries.wsu.edu/esploro/outputs/journalArticle/Rethinking-the-Use-of-Tests-A/99900601154801842) [V for k/conclusion]. Overall g reported as 0.61 (fixed effects) / 0.70 (random effects); classroom studies g = 0.67 vs lab g = 0.62; strongest at secondary level — [WSU summary PDF](https://education.wsu.edu/documents/2018/01/rethinking-use-tests.pdf/); [Marshall Memo 690](https://marshallmemo.com/issues/e94345e493c58c980e1cb6118888fab7/MarshMemo690.pdf) [secondary summaries; subgroup figures not checked against paper's tables]. Note: sources disagree on N (15,472 vs 15,427) and article count (118 vs 188).
- **Agarwal, Nunes & Blunt 2021 (Educational Psychology Review 33:1409–1453)**: screened ~2,000 abstracts, coded 50 experiments run in real schools/classrooms, 49 effect sizes, total n = 5,374; 57% showed medium or large benefits; nearly all classroom conditions (formats, timings) yielded benefit — [x-mol record](https://www.x-mol.com/paper/1371679026699517952); [RetrievalPractice.org database](https://retrievalpractice.org/review) [V]. Most studies were secondary or undergraduate; per TES none were in primary schools — [TES](https://www.tes.com/magazine/teaching-learning/general/why-retrieval-practice-safe-bet-teachers) [V, secondary report].
- **Spacing**: Cepeda et al. 2006 (Psychological Bulletin) meta-analysis of distributed practice found robust spacing benefits across 317 experiments (839 assessments), optimal gap scaling with retention interval — [doi 10.1037/0033-2909.132.3.354](https://doi.org/10.1037/0033-2909.132.3.354) [UNVERIFIED counts]. Mostly lab, verbal materials, adults. Classroom spacing evidence is smaller (e.g., spaced maths homework).
- **Interleaving**: Rohrer, Dedrick, Hartwig & Cheung 2020 (Journal of Educational Psychology) — cluster RCT in 54 US 7th-grade maths classes; interleaved practice assignments produced d ≈ 0.83 on an unannounced test one month later — [doi 10.1037/edu0000367](https://doi.org/10.1037/edu0000367) [UNVERIFIED]. Brunmair & Richter 2019 (Psychological Bulletin) meta-analysis: overall g ≈ 0.42, but benefits concentrated on discriminating similar categories (paintings, maths problem types); null or negative for word lists/expository text — [doi 10.1037/bul0000209](https://doi.org/10.1037/bul0000209) [UNVERIFIED].
- **Ten-technique meta-analysis**: Donoghue & Hattie 2021 (Frontiers in Education) meta-analysed Dunlosky's ten techniques, broadly reproducing practice testing and distributed practice at the top — [Frontiers 10.3389/feduc.2021.581216](https://www.frontiersin.org/articles/10.3389/feduc.2021.581216/pdf) [V that paper exists; effect sizes UNVERIFIED].
- **Worked examples**: Kirschner, Sweller & Clark 2006 summarize the worked-example effect as one of the best-established findings of cognitive load theory, strongest for novices, reversing for experts ("expertise reversal") — [doi 10.1207/s15326985ep4102_1](https://doi.org/10.1207/s15326985ep4102_1) [UNVERIFIED wording]. Barbieri et al. 2023 (Educational Psychology Review) meta-analysis of worked examples in maths: g ≈ 0.48 — [doi 10.1007/s10648-023-09745-1](https://doi.org/10.1007/s10648-023-09745-1) [UNVERIFIED].
- **Self-explanation**: Bisra et al. 2018 (Educational Psychology Review) meta-analysis, 64 reports, g ≈ 0.55 for prompted self-explanation — [doi 10.1007/s10648-018-9434-x](https://doi.org/10.1007/s10648-018-9434-x) [UNVERIFIED].
- **Rosenshine's Principles of Instruction (2012, American Educator)**: ten principles (daily review, small steps, many questions, models/worked examples, guided practice, checking understanding, high success rate ~80%, scaffolds, independent practice, weekly/monthly review) synthesised from cognitive science, master-teacher observation and process–product studies; it is a narrative synthesis, not a meta-analysis, so has no pooled effect size — [AFT PDF](https://www.aft.org/sites/default/files/Rosenshine.pdf) [UNVERIFIED URL path; document is widely hosted by AFT].

### Inferences
- Retrieval practice and spaced review are the single most attractive "MIP-ready" strategies: zero devices, zero cost, chalk-and-slate or oral quizzes suffice, 2–8 weeks is enough to see effects on unit tests (most classroom studies are weeks long), and nothing in the mechanism depends on language or culture. Main risk: teachers turning quizzes into high-stakes graded tests (the evidence is for low-stakes, feedback-accompanied retrieval).
- Expect real-classroom effects on standardized tests much smaller than the 0.6–0.7 lab/researcher-test averages; 0.1–0.3 SD on unit tests is a more defensible planning assumption.
- Interleaving fits maths revision in middle/high school (mix problem types in homework) — very low cost; less useful in primary early-skill acquisition where students lack the component skills.
- Worked examples + "faded" practice suit Indian classrooms with large class sizes and textbook-led teaching; low controversy.
- The base is almost entirely high-income; LMIC transfer is plausible on mechanism but not directly tested at scale.

### Gaps
- No verified primary-school classroom RCT of retrieval practice found this session.
- No LMIC or Indian classroom trials of retrieval/spacing/interleaving located (not searched exhaustively).
- Exact moderator tables of Adesope 2017 (classroom vs lab, by grade) not checked against the paper.

## 2. Explicit/direct instruction vs inquiry/discovery learning

### Takeaway
Explicit, structured instruction outperforms minimally guided discovery for novices; guided/"enhanced" discovery with scaffolds, feedback and worked examples performs well. The live debate is about pure vs guided inquiry, not explicit vs any active learning. Scripted Direct Instruction programmes have moderate-to-large effects but come from a developer-linked literature.

### Cited Findings
- **Stockard, Wood, Coughlin & Khoury 2018 (Review of Educational Research 88(4):479–507)**: 328 studies, 413 designs, ~4,000 effects, 1966–2016; effects "educationally significant, moderate to large"; all significant except affective-outcome metaregressions; publication, method and sample characteristics not systematically related to effects; little decline at maintenance; larger effects with more exposure — [U. Oregon ScholarsBank](https://scholarsbank.uoregon.edu/items/9c5db747-52be-4bd8-8771-b05d0f7c0b01/full); [Texas LD Center summary](https://texasldcenter.org/?p=360) [V]. Commonly cited average d ≈ 0.60 for reading, maths and other academic outcomes [UNVERIFIED exact value]. Caveat: Stockard's later outputs cite 377 or 500+ studies, so counts vary across her publications — same sources [V]. Stockard is affiliated with the National Institute for Direct Instruction (developer-adjacent) [UNVERIFIED affiliation; check paper disclosure].
- **Kirschner, Sweller & Clark 2006 (Educational Psychologist 41(2))**: argue minimally guided instruction (discovery, problem-based, experiential, inquiry) is less effective and efficient than guidance for novices because of working-memory limits — [doi 10.1207/s15326985ep4102_1](https://doi.org/10.1207/s15326985ep4102_1) [UNVERIFIED citation details]. Rebuttal: Hmelo-Silver, Duncan & Chinn 2007 argued PBL and inquiry, as practised, are heavily scaffolded and not "minimally guided" — [doi 10.1080/00461520701263368](https://doi.org/10.1080/00461520701263368) [UNVERIFIED].
- **Alfieri, Brooks, Aldrich & Tenenbaum 2011 (Journal of Educational Psychology, 164 studies)**: explicit instruction beat unassisted discovery, d = −0.38 (discovery minus explicit); enhanced discovery (feedback, worked examples, scaffolding, elicited explanations) beat other instruction, d = 0.30, 95% CI [0.23, 0.36] — [Texas LD Center summary](https://texasldcenter.org/?p=364); [paper PDF, Utrecht FIsme](https://www.fisme.science.uu.nl/publicaties/literatuur/2011_alfieri_discovery_learning.pdf) [V]. Critics note 17 enhanced-discovery comparisons were negative and comparison conditions are often unclear — [Filling the Pail](https://fillingthepail.substack.com/p/is-structured-or-enhanced-discovery) [V, partisan blog].
- LMIC: the Global Education Evidence Advisory Panel ("Smart Buys", World Bank/FCDO/UNICEF) rates **structured pedagogy** (structured lesson plans + materials + teacher coaching) and **teaching at the right level** among the most cost-effective approaches in low- and middle-income countries — [World Bank GEEAP](https://www.worldbank.org/en/topic/teachingandlearning/publication/cost-effective-approaches-to-improve-global-learning) [UNVERIFIED ratings wording].

### Inferences
- For Indian government schools (multi-grade, large classes, many students below grade level), explicit modelling + guided practice + checks for understanding (Rosenshine-style) is low-risk and low-cost. Full scripted DI programmes (e.g., SRA Reading Mastery) are not MIP-sized and need licensed materials; the *practices* (clear modelling, choral response, frequent checks, mastery before moving on) are adoptable within weeks.
- "Inquiry vs explicit" is controversial in the discourse (and in teacher-training ideology); MIPs framed as "guided inquiry with worked examples and scaffolds" sidestep the controversy and match Alfieri's positive finding.

### Gaps
- Stockard 2018's exact pooled d values not verified.
- No Indian-specific trials of explicit instruction practices located this session (structured pedagogy evidence is mostly sub-Saharan Africa, e.g., Kenya Tusome).

## 3. Formative assessment and feedback

### Takeaway
Feedback has robust positive average effects (~0.5 SD in meta-analyses, larger for task/process-focused information-rich feedback) but enormous heterogeneity; the famous 0.4–0.7 "formative assessment" range from Black & Wiliam is not a meta-analytic estimate, and the best quantitative re-estimate is ~0.20 SD.

### Cited Findings
- **Black & Wiliam 1998** ("Inside the Black Box"/"Assessment and Classroom Learning") is often cited for 0.4–0.7 SD gains, but the authors did not apply quantitative meta-analytic techniques — [Kingston & Nash critique literature summary, PARE](https://openpublishing.library.umass.edu/pare/article/id/1424) [V].
- **Kingston & Nash 2011 (Educational Measurement: Issues and Practice)**: 13 studies meeting criteria, 42 effect sizes; median 0.25, random-effects mean 0.20; erratum (2015) corrected some effect sizes but still states the overall effect is about 0.20 — [NCME erratum PDF](https://ncme.org/wp-content/uploads/2025/10/Erratum_to_Kingston___Nash__Vol__34__No__2_.pdf); [CU Boulder (Briggs et al. 2012)](https://colorado.edu/education/node/97/attachment/newest) [V]. Briggs, Ruiz-Primo, Furtak, Shepard & Yin 2012 criticized study selection, bias in effect sizes, and outcome-measure dependence, concluding "considerable uncertainty" remains — [CU Boulder record](https://vivo-cub.colorado.edu/display/pubid_97215) [V]. McMillan, Venable & Varier 2013 similarly — [PARE](https://openpublishing.library.umass.edu/pare/article/id/1424) [V].
- **Wisniewski, Zierer & Hattie 2020 (Frontiers in Psychology 10:3087)**: 435 studies; d = 0.48 overall with significant heterogeneity — "feedback cannot be understood as a single consistent form of treatment"; information content strongly moderates impact; larger effects on cognitive and motor outcomes than on motivational/behavioural — [PMC6987456](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC6987456) [V]. Effect-size count reported inconsistently (994 effects / >61,000 participants per a library record) [count UNVERIFIED]. Lower than Hattie & Timperley 2007's 0.79 synthesis — [critical blog](https://visablelearning.blogspot.com/p/feedback-revisited.html) [V, partisan].
- Kluger & DeNisi 1996 (Psychological Bulletin): feedback interventions averaged d ≈ 0.41 but over one-third of effects were negative, especially feedback directed at the self/person — [doi 10.1037/0033-2909.119.2.254](https://doi.org/10.1037/0033-2909.119.2.254) [UNVERIFIED].
- EEF Toolkit lists feedback as high impact for very low cost (+6 months per one guide; +8 per another) — [Structural Learning guide](https://www.structural-learning.com/post/eef-teaching-learning-toolkit-guide) [V that secondary sources conflict; check EEF site directly].

### Inferences
- MIP-friendly forms: exit tickets/slates with immediate whole-class re-teaching; task-specific oral/written feedback ("what to do next"); hinge questions; self/peer checking against a model answer. All zero-device.
- Avoid praise-of-person and grade-only feedback (negative-effect risk). Large Indian classes (40–60+) make individual written feedback costly — whole-class feedback and peer feedback are more realistic.
- Plan on 0.1–0.2 SD gains on standardized measures, not 0.5–0.7.

### Gaps
- Could not access EEF strand pages directly (403); months figures are from secondary guides.
- No LMIC-specific formative assessment meta-analysis verified this session.

## 4. Mastery learning, cooperative learning, peer tutoring/reciprocal teaching, metacognition

### Takeaway
All four have positive meta-analytic averages, but effect sizes shrink with standardized outcomes and longer studies. Reciprocal teaching and metacognition/self-regulation have among the best cost-effectiveness ratings (EEF ~+7–8 months for metacognition); peer tutoring is the most LMIC-relevant for large classes.

### Cited Findings
- **Mastery learning — Kulik, Kulik & Bangert-Drowns 1990 (Review of Educational Research 60(2))**: 108 controlled studies, mean ES ≈ 0.52; larger for weaker students and teacher-made tests — [doi 10.3102/00346543060002265](https://doi.org/10.3102/00346543060002265) [UNVERIFIED]. Counter: Slavin 1987 best-evidence synthesis found group-based mastery learning effects near zero (~0.04) on standardized tests in longer-duration studies — [doi 10.3102/00346543057002175](https://doi.org/10.3102/00346543057002175) [UNVERIFIED].
- **Cooperative learning**: Johnson, Johnson & Stanne 2000 meta-analysis of eight cooperative methods found all positive, Learning Together largest; Slavin's syntheses emphasize that group goals plus individual accountability are necessary — [Johnson et al. 2000 summary, not located this session] [UNVERIFIED]. Kyndt et al. 2013 (Educational Research Review) meta-analysis: d ≈ 0.54 on achievement — [doi 10.1016/j.edurev.2013.02.002](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.edurev.2013.02.002) [UNVERIFIED].
- **Reciprocal teaching — Rosenshine & Meister 1994 (Review of Educational Research 64(4))**: 16 studies; median effect 0.32 on standardized reading comprehension tests vs 0.88 on experimenter-developed comprehension tests — [doi 10.3102/00346543064004479](https://doi.org/10.3102/00346543064004479) [UNVERIFIED]. Original method: Palincsar & Brown 1984 (summarize, question, clarify, predict) — [doi 10.1207/s1532690xci0102_1](https://doi.org/10.1207/s1532690xci0102_1) [UNVERIFIED].
- **Peer tutoring**: Cohen, Kulik & Kulik 1982 meta-analysis of 65 school tutoring programmes: tutee effect ≈ 0.40 — [doi 10.3102/00028312019002237](https://doi.org/10.3102/00028312019002237) [UNVERIFIED].
- **Metacognition and self-regulation**: EEF May 2025 update raised the estimated impact from 7 to 8 additional months; an older EEF blog had reported a move from +8 to +7, so EEF figures shift between updates — [EEF blog](https://educationendowmentfoundation.org.uk/news/latest-updates-to-the-toolkit/); [SecEd](https://www.sec-ed.co.uk/content/news/eef-update-teaching-learning-toolkit) [V that figures conflict]. Dignath & Büttner 2008 meta-analysis: self-regulated learning training ES ≈ 0.61 (primary) and 0.54 (secondary) — [doi 10.1007/s11409-008-9029-x](https://doi.org/10.1007/s11409-008-9029-x) [UNVERIFIED].

### Inferences
- Reciprocal teaching is well suited to an MIP: 4 strategy cards, small groups, works in any language including Hindi/regional-medium texts; plan on ~0.3 SD on standardized comprehension, not 0.88.
- Structured peer tutoring (older-to-younger, or paired reading) is attractive in multi-grade and large Indian classrooms; cost is teacher time to train tutors.
- Mastery learning in a 2–8 week MIP is feasible as "unit-test → corrective → retest" cycles but the Slavin critique means gains on external tests are uncertain; also overlaps with Teaching-at-the-Right-Level (TaRL) logic, which has strong Indian RCT evidence (covered by other researchers).
- Cooperative learning only works with group goals + individual accountability; unstructured "group work" is the common failure mode.

### Gaps
- Johnson & Johnson pooled figures and Slavin's cooperative learning estimates not verified.
- Peer tutoring LMIC trials (e.g., India, Chile) not searched this session.

## 5. Early reading and early maths

### Takeaway
Systematic phonics has consistent positive effects on decoding in early grades (~0.4 SD in NRP/Ehri), smaller (~0.27) in stricter RCT-only re-analyses, with weak evidence on comprehension and no clear winner among phonics approaches. For Indian scripts (alphasyllabic aksharas), "phonics" translates to systematic akshara/sound instruction; portability of English-language effect sizes is an inference. Early maths evidence favours concrete manipulatives (small-moderate) and structured number-sense work.

### Cited Findings
- **National Reading Panel 2000 / Ehri et al. 2001 (Review of Educational Research 71(3))**: 38 studies, 66 comparisons; systematic phonics d = 0.41 overall; larger in kindergarten/grade 1 (~0.55) than later grades; weaker on comprehension for older readers — [doi 10.3102/00346543071003393](https://doi.org/10.3102/00346543071003393) [UNVERIFIED figures].
- **Torgerson, Brooks & Hall 2006 (UK DfES)**: RCT-only meta-analysis (14 RCTs) found d ≈ 0.27 for word reading accuracy, with comprehension effect not statistically significant [UNVERIFIED]; Johnston critique: mean effect remains low (0.28) when additional data are included — [Dyslexia.com reference page](https://www.dyslexia.com/reference/torgerson-brooks-2019/) [V for 0.28 critique].
- **Torgerson, Brooks, Gascoine & Higgins 2019 (Research Papers in Education 34(2):208–238)**: tertiary review of 12 systematic reviews covering 452 studies; phonics of some kind has a tentative positive effect; evidence not clear on which phonics approach is best; design and publication bias would tend to exaggerate benefits; not enough evidence for "phonics only" policy; balanced instruction indicated — [Durham repository](https://durham-repository.worktribe.com/output/1339781); [Education Uncovered](https://educationuncovered.co.uk/news/little-evidence-in-favour-of-systematic-synthetic-phonics-research-review-concludes) [V]. This is controversial in the UK "synthetic phonics" debate.
- EEF: phonics and reading comprehension strategies both effective literacy strategies; reading comprehension strategies +6 months at low cost per secondary guide — [EEF blog](https://educationendowmentfoundation.org.uk/news/latest-updates-to-the-toolkit/); [Structural Learning](https://www.structural-learning.com/post/eef-teaching-learning-toolkit-guide) [V conclusion; months UNVERIFIED]. Oral language interventions were among ten strands updated in May 2025 — [EEF](https://educationendowmentfoundation.org.uk/news/updates-ten-strands-toolkit) [V that it was updated; months not obtained, page returned 403].
- **Read-alouds/shared reading**: Mol, Bus & de Jong 2009 (Review of Educational Research) meta-analysis of interactive book reading: d ≈ 0.28 on oral language for preschool/kindergarten, smaller for older children — [doi 10.3102/0034654309332561](https://doi.org/10.3102/0034654309332561) [UNVERIFIED].
- **Concrete manipulatives / CPA**: Carbonneau, Marley & Selig 2013 (Journal of Educational Psychology): 55 studies, manipulatives vs abstract-only d ≈ 0.37 (small-moderate), moderated by instructional guidance and perceptual richness (bland objects better) — [doi 10.1037/a0031084](https://doi.org/10.1037/a0031084) [UNVERIFIED]. Fyfe et al. 2014 review supports "concreteness fading" (concrete → pictorial → abstract) — [doi 10.1007/s10648-014-9249-3](https://doi.org/10.1007/s10648-014-9249-3) [UNVERIFIED].

### Inferences
- Highly MIP-relevant and low-cost: daily 15–20 min systematic akshara/phoneme practice with decodable text; read-aloud with dialogic questioning; reciprocal teaching for grades 4+; manipulatives from local objects (sticks, bundles, stones) for place value.
- Indian evidence on structured early literacy and TaRL (Pratham/J-PAL) likely carries more weight for this project than English-phonics meta-analyses; other researchers cover it.
- Phonics is less controversial than in the UK/US if framed as "systematic sound-symbol instruction within a balanced programme".

### Gaps
- No meta-analysis of phonics in alphasyllabic Indian scripts located this session.
- No verified effect sizes for number-sense or maths-fluency interventions (e.g., Jordan et al. number-sense trials; EEF maths guidance) this session.

## 6. Project-based and problem-based learning

### Takeaway
Recent well-designed US RCTs (Lucas Education Research) show positive effects of *highly structured, curriculum-embedded* PBL with extensive teacher PD (e.g., 0.48 SD social studies in grade 2), but these packages are resource-intensive; meta-analytic averages (~0.7) come from weaker designs, and an EEF UK trial found no attainment effect. Not a good candidate for a short, low-cost MIP aiming at measurable learning gains.

### Cited Findings
- **Project PLACE (Duke, Halvorsen et al.)**: cluster RCT, grade 2, low-SES Michigan districts; PBL group gained ES = 0.48 in social studies and 0.18 in informational reading; teachers received a workshop, follow-up sessions, coaching and four PBL units totalling 80 lessons — [Edutopia](https://www.edutopia.org/article/new-study-shows-impact-pbl-student-achievement-nell-duke-anne-lise-halvorsen) [V]; restated in 2021 as 5–6 months extra learning in social studies and 2 months in informational reading — [U. Michigan news](https://news.umich.edu/project-based-learning-yields-better-student-outcomes-in-studies-supported-by-famous-filmmakers-foundation) [V].
- **Knowledge in Action AP PBL (Saavedra et al. 2021)**: cluster RCT in ~68–74 schools, AP US Government and AP Environmental Science; PBL students outperformed on AP exams; registry notes attrition correlated with treatment — [AEA RCT registry](https://www.socialscienceregistry.org/trials/2189/history/199954/changes); [U. Michigan news](https://news.umich.edu/project-based-learning-yields-better-student-outcomes-in-studies-supported-by-famous-filmmakers-foundation) [V]. "~8 percentage point" higher pass rate figure comes from an unofficial aggregator — [eduskill](https://eduskill.pages.dev/en/research/pbl-deep) [UNVERIFIED].
- **Chen & Yang 2019 (Educational Research Review 26:71–81)**: 30 studies, PBL vs traditional instruction, overall d ≈ 0.71 on academic achievement — [doi 10.1016/j.edurev.2018.11.001](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.edurev.2018.11.001) [UNVERIFIED].
- EEF project-based learning trial (Menzies et al. 2016, UK secondary, ~24 schools): no evidence of impact on literacy; possible negative effect for disadvantaged (FSM) pupils; high dropout — [EEF project page](https://educationendowmentfoundation.org.uk/projects-and-evaluation/projects/project-based-learning) [UNVERIFIED].
- All Lucas studies were funded by the George Lucas Educational Foundation, a PBL advocate — [U. Michigan news](https://news.umich.edu/project-based-learning-yields-better-student-outcomes-in-studies-supported-by-famous-filmmakers-foundation) [V for funding].

### Inferences
- PBL effects depend on heavy scaffolding, purpose-built units and months of PD — exactly what a 2–8 week, teacher-led, low-cost MIP lacks. Mixed evidence + equity concern (EEF) + advocacy funding = controversial for this purpose.
- If MIPs want PBL-flavoured work, frame it as a short, structured mini-project after explicit teaching of content, with knowledge outcomes measured.

### Gaps
- Exact AP effect size not verified; no LMIC PBL RCTs located.

## 7. Social-emotional learning and growth mindset

### Takeaway
Universal SEL programmes show reliable moderate effects on SEL skills and behaviour and smaller academic effects (Durlak 2011: +11 percentile points); most are multi-month packaged US programmes. Growth-mindset interventions have very small average effects (Sisk 2018: "weak"), with modest benefits for low-achieving/low-SES students in the best US trial and nulls in an Argentine scale-up. Both are poor bets for "significant, non-controversial" academic gains in a short MIP.

### Cited Findings
- **Durlak et al. 2011 (Child Development 82(1))**: 213 school-based universal SEL programmes, ~270,000 K–12 students; academic achievement +11 percentile points (ES ≈ 0.27) — [Future-Ed](https://www.future-ed.org/parsing-the-evidence-on-social-emotional-learnings-impact/) [V for 11 points]; [doi 10.1111/j.1467-8624.2010.01564.x](https://doi.org/10.1111/j.1467-8624.2010.01564.x) [ES 0.27 and counts UNVERIFIED].
- **Cipriano et al. 2023 (Child Development 94(5):1181–1204)**: 424 studies, 53 countries, 252 universal school-based SEL interventions, 575,361 K–12 students, studies 2008–2020; SEL improved academic achievement, skills, attitudes, behaviours, peer relationships vs controls; outcomes moderated by content, implementation quality, context — [OUP abstract](https://academic.oup.com/chidev/article-abstract/94/5/1181/8255238) [V]. Academic achievement effect g ≈ 0.22 [UNVERIFIED]. Future-Ed commentary questions whether included studies would survive independent scrutiny — [Future-Ed](https://www.future-ed.org/parsing-the-evidence-on-social-emotional-learnings-impact/) [V].
- **Sisk et al. 2018 (Psychological Science 29(4):549–571)**: Meta-analysis 1 (k = 273, N = 365,915) on mindset–achievement correlation; Meta-analysis 2 (k = 43, N = 57,155) on mindset interventions; "overall effects were weak for both meta-analyses"; some support that low-SES or academically at-risk students might benefit — [Sisk et al. PDF (abstract read directly)](https://englelab.gatech.edu/articles/2018/Sisk,%20Burgoyne%20et%20al.%20(2018)%20-%20Mindset%20and%20Academic%20Achievement.pdf) [V]. Commonly cited pooled values r ≈ 0.10 and intervention d ≈ 0.08 [UNVERIFIED; not in abstract].
- **Yeager et al. 2019 (Nature 573:364–369)**: US national probability sample of 9th graders; <1 hour online intervention raised GPA by ~0.10 grade points among lower-achieving students, with almost no academic effect for higher achievers — [UT repository](https://repositories.lib.utexas.edu/handle/2152/75735); [EdWeek](https://www.edweek.org/ew/articles/2019/08/07/national-study-shows-how-a-simple-growth.html) [V]. Yeager & Dweck argue small effects are meaningful relative to realistic benchmarks — [Progress-focused summary](https://www.progressfocused.com/2021/01/answers-to-four-critical-questions.html) [V, secondary].
- Macnamara & Burgoyne 2023 (Psychological Bulletin) reported near-zero effects after bias correction and flagged study-quality issues, while Burnette et al. 2023 (Psychological Bulletin) found small positive effects concentrated in at-risk groups — [Burnette et al. 2022 preprint on Scribd](https://www.scribd.com/document/638267878/Burnetteetal-2022) [UNVERIFIED details]. Ganimian 2020 (Educational Evaluation and Policy Analysis) found no effect of a growth-mindset intervention at scale in Argentina (LMIC) [UNVERIFIED; doi not checked].

### Inferences
- SEL: worth including only for wellbeing/behaviour goals; academic gains require long, packaged programmes, so 2–8 week teacher MIPs are unlikely to move test scores. Culturally, imported US SEL curricula need adaptation (Delhi's Happiness Curriculum is an Indian analogue; its evidence base not reviewed here).
- Growth mindset: low cost but tiny, contested effects; exclude as a primary achievement strategy; at most an add-on messaging component for struggling students.

### Gaps
- Cipriano 2023 academic g not verified; Sisk 2018 pooled d not verified (paper body not readable this session).
- No Indian RCTs of SEL or mindset located this session.

## 8. Myths with no support (to exclude)

### Takeaway
Learning-styles "meshing" (matching instruction to visual/auditory/kinaesthetic preferences) and Brain Gym lack supporting evidence and should be excluded from MIPs.

### Cited Findings
- **Pashler, McDaniel, Rohrer & Bjork 2008 (Psychological Science in the Public Interest 9(3))**: found virtually no studies using the crossover design needed to test meshing, and the few that did contradicted it; concluded no adequate evidence base justifies learning-styles assessments in education — [doi 10.1111/j.1539-6053.2009.01038.x](https://doi.org/10.1111/j.1539-6053.2009.01038.x) [UNVERIFIED this session; search returned nothing on it].
- **Brain Gym**: Hyatt 2007 review found the theoretical claims unsupported and the few studies methodologically weak — [doi 10.1177/07419325070280020101](https://doi.org/10.1177/07419325070280020101) [UNVERIFIED].
- Dunlosky 2013 places highlighting and rereading — the most common student study habits — in the low-utility tier — [APS](https://www.psychologicalscience.org/news/releases/which-study-strategies-make-the-grade.html) [V].

### Inferences
- Also deprioritize: rereading/highlighting as revision strategies; unstructured discovery; person-focused praise.

### Gaps
- Learning-styles myth prevalence among Indian teachers not researched.

## 9. Synthesis: shortlist for 2–8 week, low-cost teacher MIPs in Indian government schools

### Takeaway
Best bets (strong evidence, near-zero cost, no devices, portable, low controversy): retrieval practice + spaced review; explicit instruction with worked examples and checks for understanding (Rosenshine-style); task-focused formative feedback (exit tickets/slates); reciprocal teaching for comprehension; structured peer tutoring; systematic sound-symbol (akshara) instruction in early grades; CPA with local manipulatives. Lower priority: PBL, SEL packages, growth mindset. Exclude: learning styles, Brain Gym.

### Inferences
- For MIP outcome measurement, effects of 0.1–0.3 SD on a teacher-made but pre-specified unit assessment are realistic for best-bet strategies over 4–8 weeks; claims of 0.5+ should be treated sceptically.
- Combining practices (e.g., explicit teaching + daily retrieval + exit-ticket feedback) is how Rosenshine and structured-pedagogy models work and probably more robust than any single technique, though combined effects are not additive.
